14 CFR Part 43—Maintenance, Preventative Maintenance Rebuilding, and Alteration

Section 43.1—Applicability

Paragraph (a) states quite clearly that aircraft (whether U.S.- or foreign-registered operating under 14 CFR part 121 or 135) and component parts thereof must be maintained in accordance with the rules set forth in this part. Although paragraph b states quite clearly the type of aircraft that this part does not apply to, it seems to have led to considerable confusion within the aviation industry. If an aircraft is flying with a Special Airworthiness—Experimental Certificate (FAA Form 8130-7, Special Airworthiness Certificate—pink color certificate) and that is the only airworthiness certificate this aircraft has ever had, then 14 CFR part 43 does not apply.

Conversely, sometimes during maintenance (especially STC modification—the STC is addressed later in this chapter), it becomes necessary to temporarily place the aircraft into Special Airworthiness—Experimental. This is done to show compliance with federal regulations. These aircraft must still be maintained in accordance with 14 CFR part 43, because the aircraft had a different kind of airworthiness (in this example a Standard) prior to being issued the Special Airworthiness Certificate.

Section 43.2—Records of Overhaul and Rebuilding

These terms are not defined in 14 CFR part 1 and are given full explanation in this subpart. Each term states that it may not be used to describe work done on an aircraft, airframe, aircraft engine, propeller, appliance, or component part unless that item has been:

• Repaired, as necessary

• Tested

The key difference between the two terms is in determining how the item is tested. If it is “tested in accordance with approved standards acceptable to the Administration that have been developed and documented by the manufacturer, the item is said to be overhauled.” This is basically another way of describing “service limits,” a term frequently used to describe manufacturer specified acceptable limits for used parts. A “rebuilt item, on the other hand, must be tested to the same tolerances and limits as a new item.”

Section 43.3—Persons authorized to perform maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, and alterations

There are nine different persons who may perform maintenance: (Reminder: Per 14 CFR part 1, the FAA definition of a person is “an individual, firm, partnership, corporation, association, joint-stock association, or governmental entity. It includes a trustee, receiver, assignee, or similar representative of any of them.”)

  1. Certificated mechanic, per 14 CFR part 65
  1. Certificated repairman, per 14 CFR part 65
  1. Person working under the supervision of a certificated mechanic or repairman
  1. Holder of repair station certificate
  1. Holder of an air carrier certificate
  1. Except for holders of a sport pilot certificate, the holder of a pilot certificate issued under part 61 may perform preventive maintenance on any aircraft owned or operated by that pilot which is not used under 14 CFR part 121, 129, or 135. The holder of a sport pilot certificate may perform preventive maintenance on an aircraft owned or operated by that pilot and issued a special airworthiness certificate in the light-sport category.
  1. Pilot of a helicopter (when operated under 14 CFR part 135 and in remote areas) may perform specific preventive maintenance actions. These actions may only be accomplished under the following conditions:

• The mechanical difficulty or malfunction occurred en route to or in the remote area.

• The pilot has been satisfactorily trained and is authorized in writing by the certificate holder to perform the required maintenance.

• There is no certificated mechanic available.

• The certificate holder has procedures to evaluate the work performed when a decision for airworthiness is required.

The work done is listed in paragraph (c) of Appendix A of this chapter.

  1. Holder of part 135 certificate may allow pilots of aircraft with nine or less passenger seats to remove and reinstall cabin seats and stretchers and cabin mounted medical oxygen bottles. These actions may only be accomplished under the following conditions:

• The pilot has been satisfactorily trained and is authorized in writing by the certificate holder to perform the required maintenance.

• The certificate holder has written procedures available to the pilot to evaluate the work performed.

  1. Manufacturer may inspect and rebuild any item it has manufactured.

Section 43.5—Approval for return to service after maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, and alterations

Approving an aircraft component for return to service after maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, or alteration must be done by creating an appropriate maintenance record entry as required by either 14 CFR part 43, section 43.9 or 43.11. This may include the use of FAA Form 337, Major Repair and Alteration, if the maintenance action was a major repair or a major alteration. Whenever a maintenance action is being planned, it is critical that the technician understands exactly:

  1. What he/she is going to do.
  1. How that work is classified by the FAA.
  1. What type of documentation is required to support this activity.

First consider whether this a repair or an alteration. This should be a relatively simply decision since a repair basically returns the aircraft to its previous or unaltered condition (i.e., replacing magnetos, an exhaust system, tires, or brakes). Even replacing an entire engine (although it is a big job) is still a repair if it is the one properly specified for that aircraft. An alteration on the other hand, always changes or modifies the aircraft from its previous state (i.e., installing winglets, new avionics, or an engine that is not listed in the aircraft TCDS). The second question to consider is whether or not the work that to be performed constitutes a major or a minor maintenance action. A “major” action is typically one that might appreciably affect weight, balance, structural strength, performance, powerplant operation, flight characteristics, or other qualities affecting airworthiness and that are not done according to accepted practices or cannot be done by elementary operations. This is a much more complex question, but it is extremely important as it drives the final question concerning the substantiating documentation. Please refer to 14 CFR part 1 and part 43, appendix A, for additional clarification and examples.

The third question deals with the type of documentation required to substantiate the work performed. Minor repairs and alterations need only to refer to “acceptable” data, such as manufacturers’ maintenance manuals or AC 43.13-1. The maintenance action can simply be recorded in the maintenance record as a logbook entry. Major repairs and alterations require “approved data.” Some examples of approved data are AD notes, STCs, TCDS, DER-specific delegations, and FAA-approved manufacturer Service Bulletins (SB). Sometimes the repair or alteration being performed does not have previously-approved data. In that case, the technician may request that the FAA accomplish a “Field Approval.” In this procedure, the technician completes the front side of Form 337 through block 6 (leaving block 3 open for later FAA approval) and then indicates in block 8 on the back what work is to be done and what the substantiating reference data is. Form 337 is then submitted to the local FAA FSDO office for review and approval by an ASI. If necessary, this ASI may seek input from other ASIs or FAA specialists to assist in the review of the data. If the data is found to comply with FAA regulations, the ASI enters one of the following statements in block 3, depending on whether the ASI has performed a review of the data only or has physically inspected the aircraft:

• “The technical data identified herein has been found to comply with applicable airworthiness requirements and is hereby approved for use only on the above described aircraft, subject to conformity inspection by a person authorized in 14 CFR part 43, section 43.7.” or

• “The alteration or repair identified herein complies with the applicable airworthiness requirements and is approved for use only on the above described aircraft, subject to conformity inspection by a person authorized in 14 CFR part 43, section 43.7.”

Section 43.7—Persons authorized to approve aircraft, airframes, aircraft engines, propellers, appliances, or component parts for return to service after maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, or alteration

There are seven different persons listed in this section who may sign RTS documentation:

  1. Certificated mechanic or holder of an inspection authorization (IA).
  1. Holder of a repair station certificate.
  1. Holder of an air carrier certificate.
  1. Certificated private pilot.
  1. Repairman certificated with a maintenance rating for light sport aircraft (LSA) only.
  1. Certificated sport pilot for preventive maintenance on an aircraft owned and or operated by them.

Note that although a certificated repairman is authorized to work on a product undergoing maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, or alterations (refer to 14 CFR part 43, section 43.3), they are not authorized to approve that product for RTS. They must make the appropriate maintenance record entry per the requirements of 14 CFR part 43, section 43.9 or 43.11.

Section 43.9—Content, form and disposition of maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, and alteration records (except inspection performed in accordance with parts 91 and 125, and sections 135.411(a)(1) and 135.419 of this chapter)

The first observation is that this section specifically excludes inspection entries (those are covered in 14 CFR part 43, section 43.11). This section deals exclusively with maintenance record entries. The next observation is that the list of maintenance actions includes “preventive maintenance.” As stated in the explanation of 14 CFR part 43, section 43.3, a certificated pilot is authorized to perform preventive maintenance on the aircraft they own or operate. Therefore, remember that the pilot must make a record entry of the preventive maintenance they have accomplished. There are three distinct issues to be addressed in the maintenance entry and they answer the questions of “what?, when?, and who?”

• What—a description of the work performed

• When—the date the work was completed

• Who—the name of the person who did the work if other than the person who approves the RTS —the signature, certificate number, and type of certificate of the person who is approving the work for RTS

Note: Frequently, logbooks have a statement entered that ends something like this: “ … and is hereby returned to service. Joe Fixer A&P, Certificate #123456789.” As this section of the regulation currently reads, that part of the record entry is not required. Title 14 of the CFR part 43, section 43.9 clearly states that “the signature constitutes the approval for return to service only for the work performed.” Furthermore, the technician is only signing off the work they have done. Later, 14 CFR part 43, section 43.11 explains that an inspection write-up usually carries a broader scope of responsibility. This section is very clear that the entry completed in accordance with this section only holds the technician responsible for the service maintenance action they entered.

If the maintenance accomplished was a major repair or alteration, the work must be documented on FAA Form 337 and requires supporting approved data. If the maintenance action was a major repair and it was done by a certificated repair station, a signed copy of the completed customer work order accompanied by a signed maintenance release may be used in lieu of the FAA Form 337.

Section 43.10—Disposition of Life-Limited Aircraft Parts

(Note the even number again. This regulation became part of 14 CFR part 43 in 2002.)

This section presents two terms not previously defined in 14 CFR:

  1. Life-limited part means any part that has specified a mandatory replacement limit.
  1. Life status means the accumulated cycles, hours, or any other mandatory limit of a life-limited part.

This section then goes on to specify what to do with parts that are temporarily removed from and then reinstalled on a type-certificated product; what to do with parts that are removed from a type certified product and not immediately reinstalled; and how to transfer life-limited parts from one type-certificated product to another.

When a life-limited part is removed, the person removing it from the type-certificated product must control the part and ensure proper tracking of the life-limiting factor. This is to prevent the installation of the part after it has reached its life limit. There are seven possible methods the technician or repair facility may choose from to comply with this requirement.

  1. Recordkeeping
  1. Tagging
  1. Non-permanent marking
  1. Permanent marking
  1. Segregation
  1. Mutilation
  1. Any other method approved or accepted by the FAA

When a life-limited part is transferred, the information concerning the life status of that part must be transferred with it. Although regulations already did exist that required the tracking of life-limited parts when they were installed on an aircraft, this regulation was generated to govern the disposition of such parts when they were removed from the aircraft.

Section 43.11—Content, form, and disposition of records for inspections conducted under parts 91 and 125, and sections 135.411(a)(1) and 135.419 of this chapter

This section deals exclusively with inspection record entries; however, the requirements are similar to 14 CFR part 43, section 43.9 in that information of what, when, and who is required.

• What—type of inspection, including a brief description

• When—date of the inspection and the total time in service

• Who—the signature, certificate number, and kind of certificate of the person approving or disapproving the RTS

Since this is an inspection write-up and not a maintenance entry, it is quite possible that the inspecting technician could reject or disapprove the item being inspected for the RTS. When that situation occurs, the regulation states in paragraph (b) that a list of discrepancies must be given to the owner. A reference to this list and its delivery to the aircraft owner must be reflected in the record entry. Although the regulation neither specifies how those discrepancies can be cleared, nor who may do them, any appropriately-rated repair station or certificated technician can perform the required maintenance actions. When they are completed and the proper maintenance record entries are generated in accordance with 14 CFR part 43, section 43.9, the aircraft is approved for RTS. It is neither necessary to have an additional inspection, nor is it necessary to contact the disapproving inspector.

If the aircraft is on a progressive inspection program, the inspection statement changes slightly from the statement referenced earlier by adding the reference to both a “routine inspection” and a “detailed inspection.” Refer to explanatory text of 14 CFR part 43, section 43.15 for a definition of these terms. Inspections accomplished in accordance with other inspection program requirements must identify that particular program and that part of the program the inspection completed.

Section 43.12—Maintenance Records: Falsification, Reproduction, or Alteration

The aviation community relies heavily on trust and honesty in both oral and written communication. The maintenance log entries described in 14 CFR part 43, sections 43.9 and 43.11 provide the documentation trail relied upon by aircraft owners, pilots, and technicians regarding the aircraft’s maintenance history. Falsification of these records is potentially dangerous to the personnel who rely on the accuracy of these records.

This section identifies that fraudulent entries are unacceptable. If someone commits such an act, that action is the basis for suspension or revocation of the appropriate certificate, authorization, or approval. A technician who is encouraged by their employer, or by anyone else, to falsify records in any way should remember this comment: “Companies come and go, but my signature lasts a lifetime. I will not use it inappropriately.”

Section 43.13—Performance Rules (General)

This section deals with the specific requirements for conducting maintenance. ( Note: This section best reflects the relationship between the FAA’s numbering of ACs and the regulations they are related to.) Paragraph 3 on the cover page of AC 43.13-2B, Acceptable Methods, Techniques, and Practices—Aircraft Alterations, dated March 3, 2008 states:

“Title 14 of the Code of Federal Regulations (14 CFR) part 43, section 43.13(a) states that each person performing maintenance, alteration, or preventive maintenance on an aircraft, engine, propeller, or appliance must use the methods, techniques, and practices prescribed in the current manufacturer’s maintenance manual or Instructions for Continued Airworthiness prepared by its manufacturer, or other methods techniques or practices acceptable to the Administrator, except as noted in section 43.16.” Although not all ACs are linked this directly, there is a definite relationship between ACs and companion regulations. Refer to this chapter on ACs for additional information.

Aircraft maintenance technicians (AMTs) are highly skilled personnel, because aviation maintenance work requires great attention to detail. The complexity of technology on today’s aircraft demands a significant level of communication to properly accomplish maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, or alteration. This communication frequently comes in written form (i.e., manufacturer’s maintenance manuals or ICA). If neither of these documents provide the guidance the technician needs to perform maintenance, either AC 43.13 (AC 43.13-1 or AC 43.13-2) contain examples of “other methods, techniques, or practices acceptable to the Administrator” that may be sufficient. However, these ACs specifically state that the information is applicable to non-pressurized areas of civil aircraft weighing 12,500 lb gross weight or less. In addition to the documentation, the technician must also use the proper tools, equipment, and test apparatus that ensures that the work complies with accepted industry practices. If the test equipment specified by the manufacturer is not available, equipment that is determined to be equivalent and acceptable to the Administrator may be used. The technician should be cautious, however, as “proving” the equivalence of test equipment may not be as simple as it seems.

Air carriers (commercial—“scheduled” airlines operating under 14 CFR part 121, the “commuter/on demand” aircraft operating under 14 CFR part 135, and foreign air carriers and operators of U.S.-registered aircraft under 14 CFR part 129) may use the maintenance manual required by the operations specifications to comply with the requirements of this section. The operator must provide a continuous airworthiness maintenance and inspection program acceptable to the Administrator.

Section 43.15—Additional Performance Rules for Inspections

This section presents general comments concerning the responsibility of conducting an inspection and then provides details of three separate conditions. They are rotorcraft, annual and 100-hour inspections, and progressive inspections.

  1. Rotorcraft—If a rotorcraft is being inspected, specific items, such as rotor transmissions and drive shafts, must be inspected.
  1. Annual and 100-hour inspections—When performing an annual or 100-hour inspection, a checklist must be used. This checklist may be a personal one or one from the manufacturer. Either way, it must include the scope and detail of the inspection in Appendix D. Specific engine performance is also required to be tested (or monitored) as part of RTS for an annual or 100-hour inspection. This applies whether the aircraft is reciprocating or turbine powered.
  1. Progressive inspection—If a progressive inspection is being conducted, it must be preceded by a complete aircraft inspection. ( Note: A progressive inspection is the result of breaking down the large task of conducting a major inspection into smaller tasks that can be accomplished periodically without taking the aircraft out of service for an extended period of time.) Two new definitions are also presented: “routine” and “detailed.” A routine inspection is a visual examination or check of the item, but no disassembly is required. A detailed inspection is a thorough examination of the item, including disassembly. The overhaul of a component is considered to be a detailed inspection.

If the aircraft is away from the station where inspections are normally conducted, an appropriately rated mechanic, a certificated repair station, or the manufacturer of the aircraft may perform inspections in accordance with the procedures and using the forms of the person who would otherwise perform the inspection.

Section 43.16—Airworthiness Limitations

The technician performing inspection or maintenance actions on an aircraft must be certain they have all appropriate data available. Each person performing an inspection or other maintenance specified in an Airworthiness Limitations section of a manufacturer's maintenance manual or Instructions for Continued Airworthiness shall perform the inspection or other maintenance in accordance with that section, or in accordance with operations specifications approved by the Administrator under part 121 or 135, or an inspection program approved under 14 CFR part 91, section 91.409(e). ICAs, as required by 14 CFR part 21, section 21.50, must also be consulted when available. Since 1998, the FAA has required ICAs to be generated for all major alterations that are accomplished by the field approval process. This section specifies that the technician is responsible to perform inspections or maintenance specified in an airworthiness limitation in accordance with all the preceding instructions.

Section 43.17—Maintenance, preventive maintenance, or alterations performed on U.S. aeronautical products by certain Canadian persons

This section was significantly revised in 2005, as the result of a Bilateral Aviation Safety Agreement (BASA) between the United States and Canada. This section of 14 CFR part 43 defines some terms and gives specific limitations as to what an Aircraft Maintenance Engineer (AME is the Canadian equivalent to the U.S. A&P) may do to maintain U.S.-registered aircraft located in Canada. It also provides similar limitations for an Approved Maintenance Organization. (AMO is the Canadian equivalent to the U.S.- certified repair stations.)

Appendix A—Major Alterations, Major Repairs, and Preventive Maintenance

This appendix provides a comprehensive, but not exclusive, list of subjects. For instance, paragraph (a) is titled Major Alteration, and is further subdivided as follows:

• Appliance

This same subdivision is used in paragraph (b), Major Repairs. Paragraph (c), Preventive Maintenance, identifies those maintenance actions that are defined as preventive maintenance, provided the maintenance does not involve complex assembly operations. Preventive maintenance work may be accomplished by the holder of at least a private pilot certificate provided they are the owner or operator of that aircraft, and it is not operated under 14 CFR part 121, 129, or 135.

Appendix B—Recording of Major Repairs and Major Alterations

In most cases when a major repair or alteration is accomplished, FAA Form 337, Major Repair or Alteration, is completed at least in duplicate with the original going to the aircraft owner and a copy sent to the FAA Aircraft Registration Branch in Oklahoma City where all civil aircraft information is compiled and retained. Note: Historically, the second copy was sent to the local FAA FSDO within 48 hours after RTS. This copy is reviewed by an ASI and then forwarded by the FSDO to FAA records in Oklahoma City. However, in the fall of 2005, the FAA made a significant change to this submittal process and now requires the technician to submit the Form 337 directly to the Aircraft Registration Branch in Oklahoma City. Although a third copy is not required, it makes good business sense for the technician or certified repair station to keep a copy of the work that was accomplished.

However, if a certificated (part 145) repair station completes a major repair, it may provide the customer with a signed copy of the work order and a maintenance release signed by an authorized representative of the repair station, instead of the FAA Form 337. If the major repair or alteration was done by an AME or AMO, the copy normally provided to the FAA-FSDO is sent directly to the FAA Aircraft Registration Branch. However, if extended range tanks are installed in either passenger or cargo compartments, the technician must generate a third FAA Form 337 for the modification. This copy must be placed and retained in the aircraft. (Refer to 14 CFR part 91, section 91.417(d).)

Appendix C—(Reserved)

Appendix C is reserved for future use and therefore currently contains no information.

Appendix D—Scope and Detail of Items To Be Included in Annual and 100-Hour Inspections

Some important items to consider in this appendix are:

  1. The list of items and areas to be inspected are exactly the same for an annual as a 100-hour inspection. The difference between the inspections is in who is authorized to perform and approve the aircraft for RTS following the inspection. Refer to 14 CFR part 65, section 65.95(a)(2) that states that an IA must perform an annual inspection.
  1. The aircraft and engine must be cleaned prior to conducting the inspection.
  1. Any miscellaneous item not covered in the detailed list provided must also be inspected for improper installation and operation.
  1. There are eight specific areas identified for detailed inspection. They are the fuselage hull group, cabin/ flight deck group, engine/nacelle group, landing gear group, wing/center section group, empennage assembly, propeller group, and the radio group.

Appendix E—Altimeter System Test and Inspection

This is commonly referred to as “the 411 test.” Refer to 14 CFR part 91, section 91.411 that requires that no person may operate an aircraft in controlled airspace under IFR unless the aircraft has had this test completed successfully within the preceding 24 months.) This section requires detailed testing of the static pressure system, the altimeter, and the automatic pressure altitude reporting equipment, and that the test information be recorded in the maintenance logs and on the altimeter.

Appendix F—ATC Transponder Tests and Inspections

This is commonly referred to as “the 413 test.” (Refer to 14 CFR part 91, section 91.413, which requires that no person may use a transponder unless it has had this test completed successfully within the preceding 24 months.)

This section specifies complex sets of tests, which may be accomplished either as a bench test or by using portable test equipment. Major categories of the testing required are radio reply frequency, suppression, receiver sensitivity, radio frequency peak output power, and mode S (when applicable). Upon completion of testing, proper entries must be made in the maintenance record.

14 CFR Part 91—General Operating and Flight Rules

Subpart A—General

As mentioned in the brief overview of the regulation portion earlier in this chapter, this part is actually addressing the operation of the aircraft. For example, 14 CFR part 91, section 91.7(a) states “no person may operate a civil aircraft unless it is in an airworthy condition.” We learned earlier that this term means that the aircraft conforms to its approved type design and is in condition for safe operation. When the pilot performs a preflight inspection, they are making a determination concerning the “condition for safe operation.” The pilot does not usually determine “conformity to type design” unless they perform a review of the maintenance records. However, since that is fundamental to the definition of airworthy, it is still part of their responsibility. Therefore, a professional and ethical technician wants to help the customer understand their responsibilities in maintaining and documenting the airworthiness of the aircraft.

Subpart E—Maintenance, Preventive Maintenance, and Alterations

Section 91.401—Applicability Although this subpart describes in general the rules regarding maintenance, preventive maintenance, and alteration, certain sections do not apply if the aircraft is operated in accordance with 14 CFR part 121, 125, 129, or 135.

Section 91.403—General The owner/operator holds the primary responsibility for maintaining the aircraft in airworthy condition. This includes compliance with all applicable ADs and is the reason that the FAA sends new AD notes to the registered owners of the affected aircraft. All maintenance performed must be accomplished in accordance with 14 CFR part 43. Compliance with the appropriate manufacturer maintenance manuals and ICA is also required. Mandatory replacement times, inspection intervals, and related procedures as outlined in the FAA-approved operations specifications must also be complied with.

Section 91.405—Maintenance Required The owner/operator is required to have the appropriate inspections made, and to have discrepancies repaired in accordance with part 43. They are also required to ensure that the appropriate entries have been made in the maintenance records. Any inoperative instruments or equipment must be properly placarded as inoperative.

Section 91.407—Operation after maintenance, preventive maintenance, or alteration Whenever the aircraft has undergone maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding or alteration, it must have been approved for RTS and a proper entry made in the maintenance records. If the maintenance that was done could have appreciably changed the flight characteristics, an appropriately rated pilot must perform an operational flight check of the aircraft and must make an entry of the flight in the maintenance records. If ground testing and inspection can show conclusively that the maintenance has not adversely affected the flight characteristics, no flight test is required.

Section 91.409—Inspections This paragraph identifies various types of inspection applicable to the civilian aircraft fleet. Paragraph (a) defines the requirement for an annual inspection. However, there are certain exceptions to this regulation:

  1. An aircraft that carries a special flight permit, a current experimental certificate, or a light-sport or provisional airworthiness certificate;
  1. An aircraft inspected in accordance with an approved aircraft inspection program under part 125 or 135 of this chapter and so identified by the registration number in the operations specifications of the certificate holder having the approved inspection program;
  1. An aircraft subject to the requirements of paragraph (d) or (e) of this section; or
  1. Turbine-powered rotorcraft when the operator elects to inspect that rotorcraft in accordance with paragraph (e) of this section.

Annual inspections are usually the inspection method associated with small “general aviation” aircraft. If this same aircraft is used for hire (including flight instruction for hire), then the aircraft must also be inspected every 100 hours of time in service. This requirement for a 100-hour inspection to be conducted on an aircraft may be exceeded by as much as 10 hours if the aircraft is en route to reach a facility that will be conducting the inspection. Any time accrued between 100 and 110 hours is subtracted from the hours remaining before the next 100-hour inspection.

Since aircraft used for hire only generate revenue when they are flying, any time that the aircraft is “down for inspection” can result in a loss of income for the owner/operator. Therefore, the FAA has made provision to minimize the impact of the 100-hour and annual inspection requirement. The owner/operator may petition the local FSDO for approval of a progressive inspection program. This program breaks the complete inspection of the aircraft into smaller, less time-consuming steps. (Refer to 14 CFR part 43, Appendix D.) This inspection may be either performed or supervised by a technician holding an IA. The program must ensure at all times that the aircraft is airworthy. The owner/operator must submit an inspection schedule with their application to the FAA. This schedule must identify the time intervals (hours or days) when routine and detailed inspections are to be accomplished. (Refer to 14 CFR part 43, section 43.15.) Just as with the 100-hour inspection, a 10-hour maximum extension of a specified inspection interval is allowed if the aircraft is en route. A change in the inspection interval is also allowed for changes in service experience. If the progressive inspection is discontinued, the aircraft is again subject to the traditional annual and 100-hour inspections. Other inspection programs that may be applicable to other aircraft are a continuous airworthiness inspection program and an approved aircraft inspection program (AAIP). The former program is applicable to either a part 121 or 135 carrier, but the latter program is limited to part 135 operators only. Finally, the owner/operator may use either a current inspection program recommended by the aircraft manufacturer or one established by the owner/operator and approved by the local FSDO. Any subsequent changes to that program must also be approved by the local FSDO.

There may be an instance when the operator of an aircraft wishes to change from one type of inspection program to another. In that case, the time in service, calendar times, or cycles of operation from the current program must be carried over to the subsequent program.

Section 91.411—Altimeter System and Altitude Reporting Equipment Tests and Inspections Commonly referred to as “the 411 test,” this section specifies the requirements for testing the static pressure system, each altimeter instrument, and each automatic pressure altitude reporting system every 24 calendar months. The static system must also be tested any time it has been “opened and closed,” except for the normal use of the system drain and alternate static system pressure valves. If the automatic pressure altitude reporting system of the air traffic control (ATC) transponder is either installed or subjected to maintenance actions, the system must also be tested per Appendix E of 14 CFR part 43.

Due to the inherent design and accuracy of this system, only the aircraft manufacturer, a properly-rated repair station, or a certificated airframe mechanic may perform these tests. The airframe technician may only perform the inspection and test of the static pressure system. Calibration and maintenance of related instruments is specifically prohibited to the technician by the language of 14 CFR part 65, section 65.81 and specifically allowed in 14 CFR part 145, section 145.59 for repair stations holding an instrument rating.

TSO’d items are considered to be “tested and inspected” as of the date they were manufactured. The maximum altitude that the system was tested is the maximum altitude that the aircraft can be flown instrument flight rules (IFR) in controlled airspace. Section 91.413—ATC Transponder Tests and Inspections This “413 test” is the other test required every 24 months. Whenever the ATC transponder is installed or has undergone maintenance, the complete system must be tested and inspected in accordance with Appendix E of 14 CFR part 43. The transponder itself must be tested and inspected in accordance with Appendix F of 14 CFR part 43. As with the 411 test, only certain persons are authorized to conduct the tests. They are the manufacturer of the aircraft, a properly certificated repair station, or the holder of a continuous airworthiness maintenance program under 14 CFR part 121 or 135.

Section 91.415—Changes to Aircraft Inspection Programs If the FAA determines that the inspection program established and approved under either 14 CFR part 91, section 91.409 or 91.1109 must be revised to ensure continued safety and adequacy of the program, the owner/operator must make the necessary changes as identified by the Administrator. If the owner/operator desires to contest this request, they must petition the FAA to reconsider their request to change the program within 30 days of receiving the change request from the FAA.

Section 91.417—Maintenance Records The understanding and implementation of this section is fundamental to the aircraft industry, in general, and the aircraft owner/operator, in specific. A professional maintenance technician must be knowledgeable of this section and be able to help the owner/operator understand it. This section identifies four types of records— two are quite specific (paragraphs a and d) and two are more general: (a)(1) and (a)(2). Paragraph (a) refers to the 411 and 413 testing that requires testing every 24 months. Therefore, records must be kept for that length of time. Paragraph (d) refers to the installation of fuel tanks in the cabin or cargo area. The FAA Form 337 authorizing this installation must be kept on board the aircraft all the time.

Note: Other than this paragraph, there is no requirement that the maintenance records of the aircraft be carried on the aircraft. In fact, there are very logical reasons to not do so in most cases. The two biggest concerns are damaged or lost records. It is much safer to retain the logs in a filing system in the office. It is also a very wise idea to have the logbook copied or scanned and retained at a separate location should a catastrophic event (fire, flood, tornado, hurricane, and so forth) occur at the site the original records are retained. Subparagraph (a)(1) then lists those records that are later defined in (b)(1) as being retained for 1 year or until the work is repeated or superseded. Subparagraph (a)(2) specifies the records that are permanent records and are identified in subparagraph (b)(2) as those that must be transferred with the aircraft. Paragraph (c) requires that all of the maintenance records mandated by this section be made available upon request to the Administrator or any authorized representative of the NTSB. Furthermore, the owner/operator must provide the Form 337 required to be aboard the aircraft whenever additional fuel tanks are installed in either the passenger compartment or the baggage compartment, per paragraph (d), to any law enforcement officer upon request. Section 91.419—Transfer of Maintenance Records When an aircraft is sold, it is logical that the records are transferred with it. They may be either in plain language or coded. The purchaser may elect to permit the seller to retain the actual records; however, if that occurs the purchaser (now the current owner/operator) must still make these records available to either the FAA or the NTSB upon request.

Section 91.421—Rebuilt Engine Maintenance Records This section presents the term “zero time.” Although not truly given as a definition, the wording of the regulation is very clear that an aircraft engine, when rebuilt by the engine manufacturer or an agency approved by the manufacturer, may be given a new maintenance record showing no previous operating history. This new record must include a signed statement with the date it was rebuilt, any changes incorporated by compliance with AD notes, and compliance with any of the manufacturer’s SB.

第（a）款说得相当清楚：航空器（无论是美国登记，还是按 14 CFR part 121 或 135 运行的外国登记航空器）及其部件，都必须按照本 part 规定的规则实施维修。尽管第 b 款清楚地说明了本 part 不适用的航空器类型，但它似乎在航空业内引起了相当多的困惑。如果一架航空器持有特别适航证——实验类适航证（FAA Form 8130-7，特别适航证——粉色证书），并且这是该航空器有史以来唯一持有的适航证，那么 14 CFR part 43 不适用。

第 43.2 节——翻修与重建记录

这些术语在 14 CFR part 1 中没有定义，本分部对其作了完整解释。每个术语都规定：除该物件已完成下列工作外，不得用它来描述对航空器、机身、航空器发动机、螺旋桨、机载设备或部件所实施的作业：

• 按需修理

• 试验

这两个术语的关键差别在于判定该物件是如何试验的。如果它是“按经局长可接受、由制造商制定并形成文件的批准标准进行试验，则该物件称为已翻修（overhauled）”。这基本上是用另一种方式描述“使用极限”，这个词常用来指制造商规定的二手件可接受极限。相反，“重建（rebuilt）的物件必须按与新件相同的公差和极限进行试验”。

有九类不同人员可以实施维修：（提示：按 14 CFR part 1，FAA 对 person（人）的定义是“个人、商号、合伙组织、公司、协会、股份制协会或政府实体。它包括上述任何一方的受托人、破产管理人、受让人或类似代表。”）

  1. 按 14 CFR part 65 取得证书的修理人员（repairman）
  1. 在持证维修人员或修理人员监督下工作的人
  1. 修理站证书持有人
  1. 航空承运人证书持有人
  1. 除持运动驾驶员执照者外，按 part 61 取得驾驶员执照的持有人，可对该驾驶员拥有或运行、且不按 14 CFR part 121、129 或 135 使用的任何航空器实施预防性维修。持运动驾驶员执照者，可对该驾驶员拥有或运行、且已取得轻型运动类特别适航证的航空器实施预防性维修。

  1. part 135 证书持有人可允许九座及以下航空器的驾驶员拆装客舱座椅、担架和客舱安装的医疗氧气瓶。这些操作只能在下列条件下完成：

第 43.5 节——维修、预防性维修、翻修与改装后恢复使用的批准

维修、预防性维修、翻修或改装后对航空器部件作出恢复使用（RTS）的批准，必须通过按 14 CFR part 43 第 43.9 节或第 43.11 节的要求制作相应的维修记录条目来完成。若该维修活动属重大修理或重大改装，这可能包括使用 FAA Form 337（重大修理与改装）。无论规划何种维修活动，技术人员都必须确切了解：

  1. 自己将要做什么。
  1. 该工作被 FAA 归为哪一类。
  1. 需要何种文件来支持这项活动。

首先考虑这是修理还是改装。这应当是一个相对简单的判断，因为修理基本上是把航空器恢复到其先前或未改装的状态（即更换磁电机、排气系统、轮胎或刹车）。即使更换整台发动机（尽管工作量大），只要它是该航空器正确规定的发动机，仍属修理。另一方面，改装总是改变或修改航空器使其不同于先前状态（即加装翼梢小翼、新航电，或安装未列于该航空器 TCDS 的发动机）。要考虑的第二个问题是，将实施的作业是否构成重大或次要维修活动。“重大”活动通常指可能明显影响重量、平衡、结构强度、性能、动力装置运行、飞行特性或其他影响适航性的品质，且未按公认做法进行、或无法用基本操作完成的活动。这个问题复杂得多，但极为重要，因为它决定最后一个关于证实性文件的问题。更多说明和示例请参阅 14 CFR part 1 与 part 43 附录 A。

第三个问题涉及证实所完成工作所需的文件类型。次要修理和改装只需引用“可接受（acceptable）”的数据，如制造商维修手册或 AC 43.13-1。该维修活动只需作为日志条目记入维修记录即可。重大修理和改装则要求“经批准（approved）的数据”。经批准数据的示例包括 AD notes、STCs、TCDS、DER 专项授权，以及 FAA 批准的制造商服务通告（SB）。有时所实施的修理或改装没有事先批准的数据。这种情况下，技术人员可请 FAA 实施“现场批准”（Field Approval）。在此程序中，技术人员填写 Form 337 正面直至第 6 栏（第 3 栏留空，供 FAA 日后批准），然后在背面第 8 栏说明要做什么工作以及证实所依据的参考数据。随后 Form 337 提交当地 FAA FSDO 办公室，由 ASI 审查批准。必要时，该 ASI 可征求其他 ASI 或 FAA 专家的意见以协助审查数据。若认定数据符合 FAA 规章，ASI 将根据其只是审查了数据还是实地检查了航空器，在第 3 栏填入下列语句之一：

•“本文件所述技术数据经认定符合适用的适航要求，特此批准仅用于上述航空器，但须由 14 CFR part 43 第 43.7 节授权的人员作符合性检查。”或

•“本文件所述改装或修理符合适用的适航要求，并批准仅用于上述航空器，但须由 14 CFR part 43 第 43.7 节授权的人员作符合性检查。”

第 43.7 节——有权在维修、预防性维修、翻修或改装后批准航空器、机身、航空器发动机、螺旋桨、机载设备或部件恢复使用的人员

本节列出七类可签署 RTS 文件的人员：

  1. 持证维修人员或持检查授权（IA）者。
  1. 修理站证书持有人。
  1. 航空承运人证书持有人。
  1. 持证私用驾驶员。
  1. 仅对轻型运动航空器（LSA）取得维修等级证书的修理人员。
  1. 持证运动驾驶员，对其拥有和/或运行的航空器实施预防性维修。

注意：尽管持证修理人员被授权对接受维修、预防性维修、翻修或改装的产品的作业（见 14 CFR part 43 第 43.3 节），但他们无权批准该产品恢复使用。他们必须按 14 CFR part 43 第 43.9 节或第 43.11 节的要求制作相应的维修记录条目。

第 43.9 节——维修、预防性维修、翻修与改装记录的内容、格式与处置（不含按 part 91、125 及本章第 135.411(a)(1) 节和第 135.419 节实施的检查）

首先要指出的是，本节明确排除检查记录（那些由 14 CFR part 43 第 43.11 节涵盖）。本节只涉及维修记录条目。第二点，维修活动清单中包含“预防性维修”。如 14 CFR part 43 第 43.3 节的解释所述，持证驾驶员被授权对其拥有或运行的航空器实施预防性维修。因此要记住，驾驶员必须就其已完成的预防性维修作记录条目。维修条目中要处理三个不同的问题，它们回答“做了什么？何时做的？谁做的？”。

• 做了什么——对所完成工作的描述

• 何时——工作完成的日期

• 谁——若完成工作的人不是批准恢复使用者，则为其姓名——批准该工作恢复使用者的签名、证书编号和证书类型

注意：日志中常写有这样的语句，结尾类似：“……特此恢复使用。Joe Fixer A&P，证书号 #123456789。”按该节规章现行文本，记录条目的这一部分并非必需。CFR 第 14 编 part 43 第 43.9 节明确规定“签名仅对所完成的工作构成恢复使用的批准”。此外，技术人员只对自己完成的工作签字。后文 14 CFR part 43 第 43.11 节说明，检查记录通常承担更宽的责任范围。本节非常清楚：按本节完成的条目，只让技术人员对其录入的维修活动负责。

若所完成的维修属重大修理或改装，该工作必须记录在 FAA Form 337 上，并要求有支持性的经批准数据。若该维修活动是重大修理且由持证修理站完成，则可用一份签署的完工客户工作单副本连同签署的维修放行单，代替 FAA Form 337。

第 43.10 节——寿命件航空器部件的处置

（注意又是偶数编号。该规章于 2002 年成为 14 CFR part 43 的一部分。）

本节给出两个 14 CFR 此前未定义的术语：

  1. 寿命件（life-limited part）指任何规定了强制性更换极限的部件。
  1. 寿命状态（life status）指寿命件的累计循环数、小时数或任何其他强制性极限。

本节随后规定了以下情形的处理办法：对暂时从型号合格审定产品上拆下、随后又装回的部件；对从型号合格审定产品上拆下而未立即装回的部件；以及如何将寿命件从一个型号合格审定产品转移到另一个。

拆下寿命件时，从型号合格审定产品上拆下该件的人必须控制该件，并确保对寿命限制因素作正确跟踪。这是为防止该件达到寿命极限后仍被安装。技术人员或修理机构可选择七种方法来符合这一要求。

  1. 记录保存
  1. 挂标签
  1. 非永久性标记
  1. 永久性标记
  1. 隔离
  1. 损毁
  1. FAA 批准或接受的任何其他方法

寿命件转移时，有关该件寿命状态的信息必须随之转移。尽管早已有规章要求在寿命件装机时对其进行跟踪，但本规章的出台是为规范此类部件从航空器上拆下时的处置。

第 43.11 节——按 part 91、125 及本章第 135.411(a)(1) 节和第 135.419 节实施的检查的记录的内容、格式与处置

本节只涉及检查记录条目；但其要求与 14 CFR part 43 第 43.9 节类似，都需要“做了什么、何时、谁”的信息。

• 做了什么——检查类型，含简要描述

• 何时——检查日期和总使用时间

• 谁——批准或不批准恢复使用者的人员的签名、证书编号和证书种类

由于这是检查记录而非维修条目，实施检查的技术人员完全可能拒绝或不批准受检物件恢复使用。出现这种情况时，规章第（b）款规定必须向机主提供一份缺陷清单。记录条目中必须反映对该清单的引用及其向航空器机主的交付。尽管规章既未规定这些缺陷如何才能清除，也未规定谁可以清除，但任何具有相应等级的修理站或持证技术人员都可实施所需的维修工作。当这些工作完成，并按 14 CFR part 43 第 43.9 节制作了适当的维修记录条目后，航空器即被批准恢复使用。既不需要再做一次检查，也不需要联系当初不批准的检查员。

若航空器处于渐进式检查大纲之下，检查语句与前述语句略有不同，需增加对“例行检查”和“详细检查”的引用。这两个术语的定义见 14 CFR part 43 第 43.15 节的说明文字。按其他检查大纲要求完成的检查，必须指明该特定大纲以及本次检查所完成的大纲部分。

第 43.12 节——维修记录：伪造、复制或篡改

航空界在口头和书面沟通中都高度依赖信任与诚实。14 CFR part 43 第 43.9 节和第 43.11 节所述的维修日志条目，提供了机主、驾驶员和技术人员所依赖的、关于航空器维修历史的文件链。伪造这些记录，对依赖其准确性的人员具有潜在危险。

本节明确伪造条目不可接受。若有人实施此类行为，该行为即构成吊销或撤销相应证书、授权或批准的依据。技术人员若被雇主或任何其他人怂恿以任何方式伪造记录，应记住这句话：“公司来来去去，而我的签名伴我一生。我不会不当使用它。”

第 43.13 节——施工规则（总则）

本节涉及实施维修的具体要求。（注：本节最能体现 FAA 对 AC 的编号与其所关联规章之间的关系。）2008 年 3 月 3 日发布的 AC 43.13-2B《可接受的方法、技术与做法——航空器改装》封面第 3 段指出：

“CFR 第 14 编（14 CFR）part 43 第 43.13(a) 节规定，对航空器、发动机、螺旋桨或机载设备实施维修、改装或预防性维修的每个人，都必须使用现行制造商维修手册或制造商编写的持续适航说明（ICA）中规定的方法、技术和做法，或局长可接受的其他方法、技术或做法，但第 43.16 节另有规定的除外。”虽然并非所有 AC 都如此直接地与规章挂钩，但 AC 与配套规章之间确有明确关系。详见本章关于 AC 的部分。

航空器维修技术人员（AMTs）技能高超，因为航空维修工作要求极其注重细节。当今航空器技术的复杂性，要求有相当水平的沟通才能正确完成维修、预防性维修、翻修或改装。这种沟通常以书面形式进行（即制造商维修手册或 ICA）。若这两类文件都不能提供技术人员实施维修所需的指导，AC 43.13（AC 43.13-1 或 AC 43.13-2）中有“局长可接受的其他方法、技术或做法”的示例，可能足够使用。但这些 AC 明确规定，其信息适用于最大全重 12,500 磅及以下的民用航空器非增压区域。除文件之外，技术人员还必须使用能确保工作符合公认行业做法的适当工具、设备和试验装置。若制造商规定的试验设备不可得，可使用经认定等效且为局长可接受的设备。不过技术人员应谨慎，因为“证明”试验设备等效，可能并不像看起来那么简单。

航空承运人（按 14 CFR part 121 运行的商业“定期”航空公司、按 14 CFR part 135 运行的“通勤/按需”航空器，以及按 14 CFR part 129 运行美国登记航空器的外国航空承运人和运营人）可使用运行规范所要求的维修手册来符合本节要求。运营人必须提供局长可接受的持续适航维修与检查大纲。

第 43.15 节——检查的附加施工规则

本节就实施检查的责任提出一般性意见，随后给出三种不同情形的细节。它们是旋翼航空器、年度检查与 100 小时检查，以及渐进式检查。

  1. 旋翼航空器——若检查旋翼航空器，必须检查旋翼传动、传动轴等特定项目。
  1. 年度检查与 100 小时检查——实施年度检查或 100 小时检查时必须使用检查单。检查单可以是自编的，也可以是制造商的。无论哪种，都必须包含附录 D 规定的检查范围与细节。年度检查或 100 小时检查作为恢复使用的一部分，还要求测试（或监控）发动机的特定性能。无论航空器是活塞式还是涡轮动力都适用。
  1. 渐进式检查——若实施渐进式检查，必须先进行一次完整的航空器检查。（注：渐进式检查是把重大检查这一大任务分解为若干可定期完成的小任务，从而不必让航空器长时间停场的结果。）本节还给出两个新定义：“例行（routine）”与“详细（detailed）”。例行检查是对项目的目视检查或查看，不需分解。详细检查是对项目的彻底检查，包括分解。部件的翻修被视为详细检查。

若航空器不在通常实施检查的站点，具有相应等级的维修人员、持证修理站或航空器制造商可按本应实施该检查者的程序并使用其表格实施检查。

第 43.16 节——适航限制

对航空器实施检查或维修活动的技术人员必须确认自己已备妥全部适当数据。凡实施制造商维修手册或持续适航说明的适航限制（Airworthiness Limitations）节所述检查或其他维修的每个人，都必须按该节实施该检查或其他维修，或按局长依 part 121 或 135 批准运行规范、或按依 14 CFR part 91 第 91.409(e) 节批准的检查大纲实施。按 14 CFR part 21 第 21.50 节要求提供的 ICAs，在可得时也必须查阅。自 1998 年起，FAA 要求凡通过现场批准程序实施的重大改装都必须编写 ICAs。本节规定，技术人员有责任按上述全部指示实施适航限制中规定的检查或维修。

第 43.17 节——某些加拿大人员对美国航空产品实施的维修、预防性维修或改装

本节因美国与加拿大之间的双边航空安全协定（BASA）而于 2005 年作了重大修订。14 CFR part 43 的这一节界定了一些术语，并对航空器维修工程师（AME，相当于美国的 A&P）为维护在加拿大的美国登记航空器可以做什么作了具体限制。它同时也对经批准的维修机构（AMO，相当于美国持证修理站）给出了类似限制。

14 CFR part 91——一般运行与飞行规则

第 A 分部——总则

如本章前文规章概述部分所述，本 part 实际针对的是航空器的运行。例如，14 CFR part 91 第 91.7(a) 节规定“任何人不得运行民用航空器，除非其处于适航状态”。我们前面学过，这一术语意味着航空器符合其经批准的型号设计，并处于安全运行状态。驾驶员实施飞行前检查时，就是在对“安全运行状态”作出判断。驾驶员通常不判断“符合型号设计”，除非其查阅维修记录。但由于这是适航定义的根本，它仍属其责任的一部分。因此，专业且讲职业道德的技术人员，希望帮助客户理解其在维护和记录航空器适航性方面的责任。

第 E 分部——维修、预防性维修与改装

第 91.401 节——适用性 尽管本分部总体描述了有关维修、预防性维修和改装的规则，但若航空器按 14 CFR part 121、125、129 或 135 运行，某些节不适用。

第 91.403 节——总则 机主/运营人对保持航空器适航状态负有首要责任。这包括符合所有适用 ADs，这也是 FAA 把新 AD notes 寄给受影响航空器登记机主的原因。所实施的全部维修都必须按 14 CFR part 43 完成。还必须符合相应制造商维修手册和 ICA。还须符合 FAA 批准的运行规范中规定的强制更换时间、检查间隔和相关程序。

第 91.405 节——要求的维修 机主/运营人必须按要求实施相应检查，并按 part 43 修复缺陷。他们还必须确保已在维修记录中作适当条目。任何不工作的仪表或设备都必须正确挂签标示为不工作。

第 91.407 节——维修、预防性维修或改装后的运行 每当航空器经过维修、预防性维修、翻修或改装，都必须已获批准恢复使用，并在维修记录中作适当条目。若所做维修可能明显改变飞行特性，则必须由具有相应等级的驾驶员对航空器作运行飞行检查，并须在维修记录中记录该次飞行。若地面测试和检查能确凿表明维修未对飞行特性产生不利影响，则无需飞行试验。

第 91.409 节——检查 本段指明适用于民用航空器机队的各类检查。第（a）款规定年度检查的要求。但该规章有若干例外：

  1. 持有特别飞行许可、有效实验类证书，或轻型运动类、临时适航证的航空器；
  1. 按本章 part 125 或 135 经批准的航空器检查大纲实施检查，并且其登记号已在持有该经批准检查大纲的证书持有人的运行规范中标明的航空器；
  1. 受本节第（d）或（e）款要求约束的航空器；或
  1. 当运营人选择按本节第（e）款检查涡轮动力旋翼航空器时，该旋翼航空器。

年度检查通常是与小型“通用航空”航空器相关联的检查方式。若同一航空器用于取酬（包括取酬的飞行训练），则该航空器还必须每使用 100 小时检查一次。若航空器正在飞往实施该检查的机构途中，100 小时检查的这一要求最多可超出 10 小时。100 至 110 小时之间累计的时间，从距下次 100 小时检查剩余的小时数中扣除。

由于用于取酬的航空器只有在飞行时才产生收入，因此航空器“停场检查”的任何时间都可能给机主/运营人带来收入损失。为此，FAA 已作出安排，以尽量减小 100 小时检查与年度检查要求的影响。机主/运营人可向当地 FSDO 申请批准渐进式检查大纲。该大纲把航空器的完整检查分解为更小、耗时更少的步骤。（参见 14 CFR part 43 附录 D。）该检查可由持有 IA 的技术人员实施或监督。大纲必须始终确保航空器处于适航状态。机主/运营人必须在向 FAA 申请时提交检查进度表。该进度表必须确定实施例行检查和详细检查的时间间隔（小时或天数）。（参见 14 CFR part 43 第 43.15 节。）与 100 小时检查一样，若航空器在途中，允许对规定检查间隔最多延长 10 小时。因使用经验变化，也允许变更检查间隔。若中止渐进式检查，航空器再次适用传统的年度检查和 100 小时检查。可能适用于其他航空器的其他检查大纲有持续适航检查大纲和经批准的航空器检查大纲（AAIP）。前者适用于 part 121 或 135 承运人，后者仅限 part 135 运营人使用。最后，机主/运营人可使用航空器制造商推荐的现行检查大纲，或使用由机主/运营人制定并经当地 FSDO 批准的大纲。该大纲的任何后续变更也必须经当地 FSDO 批准。

有时运营人希望从一种检查大纲转为另一种。这种情况下，现行大纲中的使用时间、日历时间或运行循环数必须结转到后续大纲。

第 91.411 节——高度表系统与高度报告设备的测试和检查 常被称为“411 测试”，本节规定了每 24 个日历月测试静压系统、每只高度表仪表和每套自动气压高度报告系统的要求。除系统排水和备用静压系统压力阀的正常使用外，静压系统每次被“打开并关闭”后也必须测试。若 ATC 应答机的自动气压高度报告系统已安装或接受了维修工作，该系统还必须按 14 CFR part 43 附录 E 测试。

由于该系统固有设计与精度，只有航空器制造商、具有相应等级的修理站或持证机体维修人员可实施这些测试。机体技术人员只能对静压系统实施检查与测试。相关仪表的校准与维修，按 14 CFR part 65 第 65.81 节的表述明确禁止由技术人员进行，而 14 CFR part 145 第 145.59 节则明确允许持仪表等级的修理站进行。

TSO 合格的物件自其制造之日起即被视为“已测试并检查”。系统受测的最大高度，即航空器在管制空域按仪表飞行规则（IFR）可飞行的最大高度。第 91.413 节——ATC 应答机测试与检查 这项“413 测试”是每 24 个月必须完成的另一项测试。每当 ATC 应答机装机或经过维修，整个系统必须按 14 CFR part 43 附录 E 测试和检查。应答机本身必须按 14 CFR part 43 附录 F 测试和检查。与 411 测试一样，只有特定人员有权实施测试。他们是航空器制造商、具有相应证书的修理站，或按 14 CFR part 121 或 135 持有持续适航维修大纲者。

第 91.415 节——航空器检查大纲的变更 若 FAA 认定按 14 CFR part 91 第 91.409 节或第 91.1109 节建立并批准的检查大纲必须修订，以确保该大纲持续安全且充分，机主/运营人必须作出局长所确定的必要变更。若机主/运营人希望对此要求提出异议，必须在收到 FAA 变更要求后 30 天内向 FAA 申请复议其变更要求。

第 91.417 节——维修记录 对本章节的理解与落实，总体上对航空业、具体上对航空器机主/运营人都具有根本意义。专业维修技术人员必须了解本节，并能够帮助机主/运营人理解它。本节规定四类记录——两类相当具体（第 a 款和第 d 款），两类较为一般：(a)(1) 和 (a)(2)。第（a）款涉及每 24 个月测试一次的 411 和 413 测试，因此记录必须保存那么长时间。第（d）款涉及在客舱或货舱区域安装燃油箱。批准该安装的 FAA Form 337 必须始终保存在航空器上。

注：除本款外，并不要求航空器维修记录随航空器携带。事实上，多数情况下不携带很有道理。两大顾虑是记录损坏或丢失。把日志保存在办公室的档案系统中要安全得多。把日志本复印或扫描并保存在另一地点也是很明智的做法，以防保存原始记录的场所发生灾难性事件（火灾、洪水、龙卷风、飓风等）。第 (a)(1) 小段随后列出那些在 (b)(1) 中被定义为保存 1 年、或保存至工作被重做或被取代的记录。第 (a)(2) 小段规定哪些是永久记录，并在 (b)(2) 中被指明为必须随航空器转移的记录。第（c）款要求，本节规定的全部维修记录，一经局长或 NTSB 任何授权代表要求，即须提供。此外，按第（d）款，每当在客舱或行李舱安装附加燃油箱时，机主/运营人必须在任何执法官员要求时提供要求随机携带的 Form 337。第 91.419 节——维修记录的转移 航空器出售时，记录随之转移是合理的。记录可用通俗语言或编码形式。买方可选择允许卖方保留实际记录；但若如此，买方（现机主/运营人）仍必须应要求向 FAA 或 NTSB 提供这些记录。

第 91.421 节——重建发动机的维修记录 本节提出“零时间（zero time）”这一术语。虽然并未真正作为定义给出，但规章措辞非常清楚：航空器发动机由发动机制造商或制造商批准的机构重建后，可获得一份显示无先前运行历史的新的维修记录。该新记录必须包含一份签署的说明，写明重建日期、因符合 AD notes 而并入的任何更改，以及对制造商任何 SB 的符合情况。

4. 语法解析

① although 让步从句 + seem to have done

原句：Although paragraph b states quite clearly the type of aircraft that this part does not apply to, it seems to have led to considerable confusion within the aviation industry. 拆解：Although 引导让步状语从句；从句中 states 的宾语 the type of aircraft 后接 that 引导的定语从句，apply to 的宾语被提前到先行词位置，故句末留空；主句 it 指代前文这一款规定，seems to have led to… 为「似乎已经导致」的完成不定式。 要点：seem to have done 表示「看起来已经发生」，比 seems to do 更强调结果已经形成。apply to 的 to 是介词，其后必须有宾语；改成定语从句时宾语由先行词承担，这是「介词悬空」的常见写法。

② Except for 前置 + 过去分词短语层层后置

原句：Except for holders of a sport pilot certificate, the holder of a pilot certificate issued under part 61 may perform preventive maintenance on any aircraft owned or operated by that pilot which is not used under 14 CFR part 121, 129, or 135. 拆解：Except for… 为排除性状语提到句首；主语 the holder of a pilot certificate 后接过去分词短语 issued under part 61 作后置定语；谓语 may perform，宾语 preventive maintenance；on any aircraft 为状语，其后 owned or operated by that pilot 为过去分词定语，which… 引导定语从句再修饰 aircraft。 要点：英语靠「名词 + 后置修饰」层层叠加，翻译时要由后向前还原为「按 part 61 颁发执照的持有人」「该驾驶员拥有或运行的、不按 part 121/129/135 使用的航空器」。which 从句紧跟 aircraft 而非 that pilot，判断修饰对象要看语义与就近原则。

③ 动名词短语作主语 + by doing 表方式

原句：Approving an aircraft component for return to service after maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, or alteration must be done by creating an appropriate maintenance record entry as required by either 14 CFR part 43, section 43.9 or 43.11. 拆解：Approving an aircraft component for return to service 为动名词短语作主语（approve sth for RTS 为其动词搭配）；after… 为时间状语；谓语 must be done；by creating… 为方式状语；as required by… 为过去分词短语作定语，修饰 entry。 要点：动名词短语作主语时谓语用单数 must be done。by doing 说明「通过何种手段完成」，与表示原因的 because of 不同。as required by 是法规文本高频压缩结构，等于 as is required by，翻译作「按……的要求」。

④ neither…nor 引导两个前置宾语从句

原句：Although the regulation neither specifies how those discrepancies can be cleared, nor who may do them, any appropriately-rated repair station or certificated technician can perform the required maintenance actions. 拆解：Although 引导让步从句，从句谓语 specifies 带两个并列宾语从句：how those discrepancies can be cleared 与 who may do them，分别由 neither 与 nor 引导并前置；主句主语 any appropriately-rated repair station or certificated technician，谓语 can perform。 要点：neither…nor 连接两个成分时位置对称，此处两个宾语从句都提到 specifies 之后并列。注意主句 or 连接的两个单数主语由 any 限定，谓语用 can perform 单数形式也无歧义。appropriately-rated 中 rated 是「具有相应等级的」，是 FAA 文本固定说法。

⑤ 同位语 + with 独立主格 + where 定语从句

原句：In most cases when a major repair or alteration is accomplished, FAA Form 337, Major Repair or Alteration, is completed at least in duplicate with the original going to the aircraft owner and a copy sent to the FAA Aircraft Registration Branch in Oklahoma City where all civil aircraft information is compiled and retained. 拆解：when 引导定语从句修饰 cases；主句主语 FAA Form 337，其后 Major Repair or Alteration 为同位语补充表格全称；谓语 is completed；with the original going… and a copy sent… 为 with 复合结构（独立主格）作伴随状语，going 与 sent 分别为现在分词与过去分词；where… 引导定语从句修饰 Oklahoma City。 要点：with + 名词 + 分词 表示伴随情况，主动关系用现在分词、被动关系用过去分词，本句 going 与 sent 正好一主一被。同位语用逗号隔开、不加连接词，是法规与表格名称的常见写法。

⑥ no requirement that + 虚拟语气

原句：Note: Other than this paragraph, there is no requirement that the maintenance records of the aircraft be carried on the aircraft. 拆解：Other than this paragraph 为介词短语作状语；there is no requirement 为存在句；that 引导同位语从句说明 requirement 的内容，从句谓语用原形 be carried，属虚拟语气。 要点：requirement、recommendation、mandate 等表要求的名词后接 that 从句时，从句谓语用 (should) + 动词原形，should 常省略，故本句是 be carried 而非 is carried。Other than 意为「除……之外」，与 except 同义但更书面。
