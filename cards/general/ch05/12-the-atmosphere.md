# The Atmosphere

## 1. 原文

Aviation is so dependent upon that category of fluids called gases and the effect of forces and pressures acting upon gases that a discussion of the subject of the atmosphere is important to the persons maintaining and repairing aircraft.

Data available about the atmosphere may determine whether a flight will succeed, or whether it will even become airborne. The various components of the air around the earth, the changes in temperatures and pressures at different levels above the earth, the properties of weather encountered by aircraft in flight, and many other detailed data are considered in the preparation of flight plans.

Pascan and Torricelli have been credited with developing the barometer, an instrument for measuring atmospheric pressure. The results of their experiments are still used today with very little improvement in design or knowledge. They determined that air has weight which changes as altitude is changed with respect to sea level. Today scientists are also interested in how the atmosphere affects the performance of the aircraft and its equipment.

### Composition of the Atmosphere

The atmosphere is a complex and ever changing mixture. Its ingredients vary from place to place and from day to day. In addition to a number of gases, it contains quantities of foreign matter such as pollen, dust, bacteria, soot, volcanic ash, spores, and dust from outer space. The composition of the air remains almost constant from sea level up to its highest level, but its density diminishes rapidly with altitude. Six miles up, for example, it is too thin to support respiration, and 12 miles up, there is not enough oxygen to support combustion, except in some specially designed turbine engine powered airplanes. At a point several hundred miles above the earth, some gas particles spray out into space, some are dragged by gravity and fall back into the ocean of air below, while others never return. Physicists disagree as to the boundaries of the outer fringes of the atmosphere. Some think it begins 240 miles above the earth and extends to 400 miles; others place its lower edge at 600 miles and its upper boundary at 6,000 miles.

There are also certain nonconformities at various levels. Between 12 and 30 miles, high solar ultraviolet radiation reacts with oxygen molecules to produce a thin curtain of ozone, a very poisonous gas without which life on earth could not exist. This ozone filters out a portion of the sun’s lethal ultraviolet rays, allowing only enough coming through to give us sunburn, kill bacteria, and prevent rickets. At 50 to 65 miles up, most of the oxygen molecules begin to break down under solar radiation into free atoms, and to form hydroxyl ions (OH) from water vapor. Also in this region, all the atoms become ionized.

Studies of the atmosphere have revealed that the temperature does not decrease uniformly with increasing altitude; instead it gets steadily colder up to a height of about 7 miles, where the rate of temperature change slows down abruptly and remains almost constant at −55° Centigrade (218° Kelvin) up to about 20 miles. Then the temperature begins to rise to a peak value of 77° Centigrade (350° Kelvin) at the 55 mile level. Thereafter it climbs steadily, reaching 2,270° Centigrade (2,543° Kelvin) at a height of 250 to 400 miles. From the 50 mile level upward, a man or any other living creature, without the protective cover of the atmosphere, would be broiled on the side facing the sun and frozen on the other.

The atmosphere is divided into concentric layers or levels. Transition through these layers is gradual and without sharply defined boundaries. However, one boundary, the tropopause, exists between the first and second layer. The tropopause is defined as the point in the atmosphere at which the decrease in temperature, with increasing altitude, abruptly ceases. The four atmosphere layers are the troposphere, stratosphere, ionosphere, and the exosphere. The upper portion of the stratosphere is often called the chemosphere or ozonosphere, and the exosphere is also known as the mesosphere.

The troposphere extends from the earth’s surface to about 35,000 ft at middle latitudes, but varies from 28,000 ft at the poles to about 54,000 ft at the equator. The troposphere is characterized by large changes in temperature and humidity and by generally turbulent conditions. Nearly all cloud formations are within the troposphere. Approximately three-fourths of the total weight of the atmosphere is within the troposphere. The stratosphere extends from the upper limits of the troposphere and the tropopause to an average altitude of 60 miles. The ionosphere ranges from the 50-mile level to a level of 300 to 600 miles. Little is known about the characteristics of the ionosphere, but it is thought that many electrical phenomena occur there. Basically, this layer is characterized by the presence of ions and free electrons, and the ionization seems to increase with altitude and in successive layers. The exosphere, or mesosphere, is the outer layer of the atmosphere. It begins at an altitude of 600 miles and extends to the limits of the atmosphere. In this layer, the temperature is fairly constant at 2,500° Kelvin, and propagation of sound is thought to be impossible due to lack of molecular substance.

### Water Content of the Atmosphere

In the troposphere, the air is rarely completely dry. It contains water vapor in one of two forms: (1) fog or (2) water vapor. Fog consists of minute droplets of water held in suspension by the air. Clouds are composed of fog. The height to which some clouds extend is a good indication of the presence of water in the atmosphere almost up to the stratosphere. The presence of water vapor in the air is quite evident, with a military F-18 doing a high-speed fly-by at nearly Mach 1. The temperature and pressure changes that occur as the airplane approaches supersonic flight cause the water vapor in the air to condense and form the vapor cloud that is visible.

As a result of evaporation, the atmosphere always contains some moisture in the form of water vapor. The moisture in the air is called the humidity of the air. Moisture does not consist of tiny particles of liquid held in suspension in the air as in the case of fog, but is an invisible vapor truly as gaseous as the air with which it mixes. Fog and humidity both affect the performance of an aircraft. In flight, at cruising power, the effects are small and receive no consideration. During takeoff, however, humidity has important effects. Two things are done to compensate for the effects of humidity on takeoff performance. Since humid air is less dense than dry air, the allowable takeoff gross weight of an aircraft is generally reduced for operation in areas that are consistently humid.

Second, because the power output of reciprocating engines is decreased by humidity, the manifold pressure may need to be increased above that recommended for takeoff in dry air in order to obtain the same power output.

Engine power output is calculated on dry air. Since water vapor is incombustible, its pressure in the atmosphere is a total loss as far as contributing to power output. The mixture of water vapor and air is drawn through the carburetor, and fuel is metered into it as though it were all air. This mixture of water vapor, air, and fuel enters the combustion chamber where it is ignited. Since the water vapor will not burn, the effective air-fuel ratio is enriched and the engine operates as though it were on an excessively rich mixture. The resulting horsepower loss under humid conditions can therefore be attributed to the loss in volumetric efficiency due to displaced air, and the incomplete combustion due to an excessively rich fuel and air mixture.

The reduction in power that can be expected from humidity is usually given in charts in the flight manual. There are several types of charts in use. Some merely show the expected reduction in power due to humidity; others show the boost in manifold pressure necessary to restore full takeoff power.

The effect of fog on the performance of an engine is very noticeable, particularly on engines with high compression ratios. Normally, some detonation will occur during acceleration, due to the high BMEP, which stands for brake mean effective pressures, developed. However, on a foggy day it is difficult to cause detonation to occur. The explanation of this lies in the fact that fog consists of particles of water that have not vaporized. When these particles enter the cylinders, they absorb a tremendous amount of heat energy in the process of vaporizing. The temperature is thus lowered, and the decrease is sufficient to prevent detonation.

Fog will generally cause a decrease in horsepower output. However, with a supercharged engine, it will be possible to use higher manifold pressures without danger of detonation.

### Atmospheric Pressure

The human body is under pressure, since it exists at the bottom of a sea of air. This pressure is due to the weight of the atmosphere. On a standard day at sea level, if a 1-in2 column of air extending to the top of the atmosphere was weighed, it would weigh 14.7 lb. That is why standard day atmospheric pressure is said to be 14.7 pounds per square inch (14.7 psi).

Since atmospheric pressure at any altitude is due to the weight of air above it, pressure decreases with increased altitude. Obviously, the total weight of air above an area at 15,000 ft would be less than the total weight of the air above an area at 10,000 ft.

Atmospheric pressure is often measured by a mercury barometer. A glass tube somewhat over 30 inches in length is sealed at one end and then filled with mercury. It is then inverted and the open end placed in a dish of mercury. Immediately, the mercury level in the inverted tube will drop a short distance, leaving a small volume of mercury vapor at nearly zero absolute pressure in the tube just above the top of the liquid mercury column. Gravity acting on the mercury in the tube will try to make the mercury run out. Atmospheric pressure pushing down on the mercury in the open container tries to make the mercury stay in the tube. At some point these two forces (gravity and atmospheric pressure) will equilibrate out and the mercury will stabilize at a certain height in the tube. Under standard day atmospheric conditions, the air in a 1 square inch column extending to the top of the atmosphere would weigh 14.7 lb. A 1-in2 column of mercury, 29.92 inches tall, would also weigh 14.7 lb. That is why 14.7 psi is equal to 29.92 "Hg.

A second means of measuring atmospheric pressure is with an aneroid barometer. This mechanical instrument is a much better choice than a mercury barometer for use on airplanes. Aneroid barometers, or altimeters, are used to indicate altitude in flight. The calibrations are made in thousands of feet rather than in psi or inches of mercury. For example, the standard pressure at sea level is 29.92 "Hg, or 14.7 psi. At 10,000 feet above sea level, standard pressure is 20.58 "Hg, or 10.10 psi. Altimeters are calibrated so that if the pressure exerted by the atmosphere is 10.10 psi, the altimeter will point to 10,000 ft.

### Atmospheric Density

Since both temperature and pressure decrease with altitude, it might appear that the density of the atmosphere would remain fairly constant with increased altitude. This is not true, however, because pressure drops more rapidly with increased altitude than does the temperature. The result is that density decreases with increased altitude.

By use of the general gas law, studied earlier, it can be shown that for a particular gas, pressure and temperature determine the density. Since standard pressure and temperatures have been associated with each altitude, the density of the air at these standard temperatures and pressures must also be considered standard. Thus, a particular atmospheric density is associated with each altitude. This gives rise to the expression “density altitude,” symbolized “Hd.” A density altitude of 15,000 ft is the altitude at which the density is the same as that considered standard for 15,000 ft. Remember, however, that density altitude is not necessarily true altitude. For example, on a day when the atmospheric pressure is higher than standard and the temperature is lower than standard, the density which is standard at 10,000 ft might occur at 12,000 ft. In this case, at an actual altitude of 12,000 ft, we have air that has the same density as standard air at 10,000 ft. Density altitude is a calculated altitude obtained by correcting pressure altitude for temperature.

#### Absolute Humidity

Absolute humidity is the actual amount of the water vapor in a mixture of air and water. It is expressed either in grams per cubic meter or pounds per cubic foot. The amount of water vapor that can be present in the air is dependent upon the temperature and pressure. The higher the temperatures, the more water vapor the air is capable of holding, assuming constant pressure. When air has all the water vapor it can hold at the prevailing temperature and pressure, it is said to be saturated.

#### Relative Humidity

Relative humidity is the ratio of the amount of water vapor actually present in the atmosphere to the amount that would be present if the air were saturated at the prevailing temperature and pressure. This ratio is usually multiplied by 100 and expressed as a percentage. Suppose, for example, that a weather report includes the information that the temperature is 75 °F and the relative humidity is 56 percent. This indicates that the air holds 56 percent of the water vapor required to saturate it at 75 °F. If the temperature drops and the absolute humidity remain constant, the relative humidity will increase. This is because less water vapor is required to saturate the air at the lower temperature.

The dew point is the temperature to which humid air must be cooled at constant pressure to become saturated. If the temperature drops below the dew point, condensation occurs. People who wear eyeglasses have experience going from cold outside air into a warm room and having moisture collect quickly on their glasses. This happens because the glasses were below the dew point temperature of the air in the room. The air immediately in contact with the glasses was cooled below its dew point temperature, and some of the water vapor was condensed out. This principle is applied in determining the dew point. A vessel is cooled until water vapor begins to condense on its surface. The temperature at which this occurs is the dew point.

Vapor pressure is the portion of atmospheric pressure that is exerted by the moisture in the air, which is expressed in tenths of an inch of mercury. The dew point for a given condition depends on the amount of water pressure present; thus, a direct relationship exists between the vapor pressure and the dew point.

### Standard Atmosphere

If the performance of an aircraft is computed, either through flight tests or wind tunnel tests, some standard reference condition must be determined first in order to compare results with those of similar tests. The conditions in the atmosphere vary continuously, and it is generally not possible to obtain exactly the same set of conditions on two different days or even on two successive flights. For this reason, a set group of standards must be used as a point of reference. The set of standard conditions presently used in the United States is known as the U.S. Standard Atmosphere.

The standard atmosphere approximates the average conditions existing at 40° latitude, and is determined on the basis of the following assumptions. The standard sea level conditions are:

Pressure at 0 altitude (P0) = 29.92 "Hg Temperature at 0 altitude (T0) = 15 °C or 59 °F Gravity at 0 altitude (G0) = 32.174 fps/s The U.S. Standard Atmosphere is in agreement with the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) Standard Atmosphere over their common altitude range. The ICAO Standard Atmosphere has been adopted as standard by most of the principal nations of the world.

## 2. 中文翻译

航空对被称为气体的一大类流体，以及作用在气体上的力与压力效应，依赖如此之深，因此讨论大气这一课题，对从事航空器维护与修理的人员十分重要。

关于大气的现有数据，可能决定一次飞行能否成功，甚至决定航空器能否离地升空。地球周围空气的各种成分、离地不同高度上温度与压力的变化、飞行中航空器所遭遇的天气特性，以及许多其他详细数据，在制定飞行计划时都要加以考虑。

帕斯卡（Pascal）与托里拆利（Torricelli）被公认为气压计的发明者，气压计是用来测量大气压力的仪器。他们实验的结果至今仍在使用，在结构设计与相关知识上几乎没有改进。他们确定，空气具有重量，而这一重量随相对于海平面的高度变化而变化。如今，科学家们同样关心大气如何影响航空器及其设备的性能。

### 大气的组成

大气是一种复杂且不断变化的混合物。它的成分因地而异，也因日而异。除若干种气体外，它还含有大量杂质，如花粉、尘埃、细菌、烟灰、火山灰、孢子以及来自外层空间的尘埃。从海平面直到最高处，空气的组成几乎保持不变，但其密度随高度上升而迅速减小。例如在 6 英里高处，空气已稀薄到无法维持呼吸；在 12 英里高处，氧气不足以维持燃烧——只有一些专门设计的涡轮发动机飞机属于例外。在离地数百英里的某处，一些气体粒子飞散进入太空，一些被重力拖回下方浩瀚的空气海洋，还有一些则一去不返。物理学家对大气外层边缘的界限意见不一。有人认为它始于离地 240 英里处，一直延伸到 400 英里；也有人把它的下界定在 600 英里，上界定在 6,000 英里。

在各个高度上还存在某些不一致的现象。在 12 至 30 英里之间，强烈的太阳紫外线辐射与氧分子发生反应，生成一层稀薄的臭氧——一种剧毒气体，没有它，地球上的生命便无法存在。这层臭氧滤掉了太阳致命紫外线的一部分，只让足够引起晒伤、杀灭细菌和预防佝偻病的量透过。在 50 至 65 英里高处，大多数氧分子开始在太阳辐射作用下分解为自由原子，并由水蒸气生成羟基离子（OH）。同样在这一区域，所有原子都变成电离状态。

对大气的研究表明，温度并不随高度上升而均匀下降；相反，它一路稳定变冷，直到约 7 英里高处，此后温度变化率骤然减慢，并在直到约 20 英里高度内几乎恒定在 −55 °C（218 K）。随后温度又开始上升，到 55 英里高度达到 77 °C（350 K）的峰值。此后它持续攀升，在 250 至 400 英里高处达到 2,270 °C（2,543 K）。从 50 英里高度往上，人或其他任何生物若没有大气层的防护，朝太阳的一面会被烤焦，另一面则会被冻僵。

大气分为若干同心层。穿过这些层是渐变的，没有分明的边界。然而第一层与第二层之间存在一个边界，即对流层顶。对流层顶定义为大气中温度随高度上升而下降的现象骤然停止的那一点。四个大气层分别是对流层、平流层、电离层和外逸层。平流层的上部常被称为化学层或臭氧层，而外逸层又称中间层。

在中纬度地区，对流层从地球表面延伸到约 35,000 ft，但在两极约为 28,000 ft，在赤道约为 54,000 ft。对流层的特征是温度与湿度变化大，且通常气流湍乱。几乎所有的云都在对流层之内。大气总重量的大约四分之三集中在对流层内。平流层从对流层上限和对流层顶起，延伸到平均 60 英里的高度。电离层从 50 英里高度延伸到 300 至 600 英里。人们对电离层的特性知之甚少，但认为那里会发生许多电学现象。这一层的基本特征是存在离子和自由电子，电离程度似乎随高度以及逐层递增。外逸层（又称中间层）是大气的最外层，始于 600 英里高度，延伸到大气的边界。在这一层，温度相当恒定，约为 2,500 K；由于缺乏分子物质，声音被认为无法传播。

### 大气中的水汽含量

在对流层中，空气很少是完全干燥的。它以两种形式之一含有水蒸气：(1) 雾，或 (2) 水蒸气。雾由悬浮在空气中的微小水滴构成。云由雾组成。某些云所能伸展的高度，很好地说明大气中几乎直到平流层都有水存在。空气中存在水蒸气这一点相当明显：一架军方 F-18 以接近马赫 1 的速度作高速低空通过。航空器接近超音速飞行时发生的温度与压力变化，使空气中的水蒸气凝结，形成可见的蒸气云。

由于蒸发的缘故，大气中总是含有一些以水蒸气形式存在的水分。空气中的水分称为空气的湿度。水分并不像雾那样由悬浮在空气中的微小液滴构成，而是一种看不见的蒸气，其气体性质与和它混合的空气完全相同。雾和湿度都影响航空器的性能。在飞行中、巡航功率下，影响很小，不予考虑。但在起飞时，湿度却有重要影响。为补偿湿度对起飞性能的影响，要采取两项措施。由于湿空气比干空气密度小，在长期潮湿的地区运行时，航空器的允许起飞全重通常要减小。

其次，由于湿度会使往复式发动机的功率输出下降，为了获得同样的功率输出，进气歧管压力可能需要提高到高于干空气中推荐的起飞值。

发动机功率输出是按干空气计算的。由于水蒸气不可燃，它在大气中的那部分压力对功率输出毫无贡献，纯属损失。水蒸气与空气的混合气被吸入汽化器，燃油按全部是空气的假定计量加入其中。这种由水蒸气、空气和燃油组成的混合气进入燃烧室并被点燃。由于水蒸气不会燃烧，有效的油气比被加浓，发动机就像在过浓混合气下工作一样。因此，潮湿条件下由此产生的马力损失，可以归因于两方面：空气被挤占造成的容积效率下降，以及油气混合气过浓造成的不完全燃烧。

湿度所能引起的功率下降，通常在飞行手册的图表中给出。在用的图表有好几种。有的只显示湿度引起的预期功率下降；有的则显示恢复满起飞功率所需的进气歧管压力提升量。

雾对发动机性能的影响十分明显，尤其是在压缩比高的发动机上。正常情况下，加速时由于产生很高的平均有效制动压力（BMEP，brake mean effective pressure），会发生一定程度的爆震。然而在雾天，却很难引起爆震。对此的解释在于：雾由尚未汽化的水滴构成。这些水滴进入气缸后，在汽化过程中吸收大量热能。温度因此降低，而降低幅度足以阻止爆震。

雾通常会使马力输出下降。不过，对于增压发动机，可以在没有爆震危险的情况下使用更高的进气歧管压力。

### 大气压力

人体处于压力之下，因为它存在于一片空气海洋的底部。这一压力来自大气的重量。在标准日的海平面，若把一根截面积 1 in2、一直延伸到大气顶部的空气柱称重，它重 14.7 lb。这就是为什么标准日的大气压力被说成 14.7 磅每平方英寸（14.7 psi）。

既然任何高度上的大气压力都来自其上方空气的重量，压力便随高度上升而减小。显然，15,000 ft 处某一区域上方的空气总重量，小于 10,000 ft 处同一区域上方的空气总重量。

大气压力常用水银气压计测量。一根长度略超过 30 英寸的玻璃管，一端封死，然后灌满水银。接着把它倒过来，开口端插入一盘水银中。倒置管中的水银面会立刻下降一小段，在水银柱顶端上方留下一小段体积、绝对压力几乎为零的水银蒸气。作用在管中水银上的重力力图使水银流出；压在开口容器水银面上的大气压力则力图使水银留在管内。到某一点，这两个力（重力和大气压力）达到平衡，水银便在管内某一高度稳定下来。在标准日大气条件下，一根截面积 1 平方英寸、延伸到大气顶部的空气柱重 14.7 lb；一根截面积 1 in2、高 29.92 英寸的水银柱同样重 14.7 lb。这就是 14.7 psi 等于 29.92 "Hg 的原因。

测量大气压力的第二种手段是无液气压计。这种机械式仪器用在飞机上，比水银气压计合适得多。无液气压计，也就是高度表，在飞行中用来指示高度。其刻度以千英尺为单位，而不是以 psi 或英寸水银柱为单位。例如，海平面的标准压力为 29.92 "Hg，即 14.7 psi；海拔 10,000 ft 处的标准压力为 20.58 "Hg，即 10.10 psi。高度表的校准使得：若大气施加的压力为 10.10 psi，高度表就指向 10,000 ft。

### 大气密度

由于温度和压力都随高度上升而下降，看起来大气密度会随高度上升而保持相当恒定。然而事实并非如此，因为压力随高度上升下降得比温度更快。结果是密度随高度上升而减小。

运用前面学过的通用气体定律可以证明，对于某种特定气体，压力和温度决定密度。既然每一高度都对应着标准压力与标准温度，那么在这些标准温度与标准压力下的空气密度也必须视为标准密度。于是，每一高度都对应一个特定的大气密度，由此产生了“密度高度”这一说法，记作“Hd”。15,000 ft 的密度高度，是指该高度上的密度与 15,000 ft 的标准密度相同。但要记住，密度高度不一定就是真实高度。例如，在某天大气压力高于标准、温度低于标准时，10,000 ft 处的标准密度可能出现在 12,000 ft。这种情况下，在 12,000 ft 的实际高度上，空气的密度与 10,000 ft 处的标准空气相同。密度高度是用温度对压力高度进行修正后算出的高度。

#### 绝对湿度

绝对湿度是空气与水混合物中水蒸气的实际含量。它可以用克每立方米或磅每立方英尺表示。空气中能够存在的水蒸气量取决于温度和压力。在压力不变的假定下，温度越高，空气所能容纳的水蒸气越多。当空气在当时的温度和压力下已容纳了它能容纳的全部水蒸气时，就说它是饱和的。

#### 相对湿度

相对湿度是大气中实际存在的水蒸气量与在当时温度和压力下空气饱和时本会存在的水蒸气量之比。这个比值通常乘以 100，用百分数表示。例如，假设一份天气报告包含这样的信息：温度为 75 °F，相对湿度为 56%。这表明空气含有在 75 °F 下使其饱和所需水蒸气的 56%。如果温度下降而绝对湿度保持不变，相对湿度就会升高。这是因为在较低温度下使空气饱和所需的水蒸气较少。

露点是潮湿空气在压力不变条件下必须被冷却到才能变成饱和的温度。如果温度降到露点以下，就会发生凝结。戴眼镜的人都有这样的经历：从寒冷的室外进入温暖的房间，镜片上很快会聚起水汽。发生这种情况，是因为镜片温度低于室内空气的露点温度。紧贴镜片的空气被冷却到其露点以下，其中一部分水蒸气便凝结出来。测定露点就应用了这一原理：把一个容器冷却，直到水蒸气开始在其表面凝结。发生凝结时的温度就是露点。

蒸气压是大气压力中由空气中水分所施加的那一部分，以英寸水银柱的十分之一为单位表示。给定条件下的露点取决于所存在的水汽压力大小，因此，蒸气压与露点之间存在直接的关系。

### 标准大气

如果要计算航空器的性能，无论是通过飞行试验还是风洞试验，都必须先确定某种标准基准状态，以便把结果与类似试验的结果作比较。大气中的状况不断变化，一般不可能在两天，甚至连续两次飞行中得到完全相同的一组条件。由于这个原因，必须采用一组固定的标准作为参照点。美国目前采用的标准条件组称为“美国标准大气”。

标准大气近似于 40° 纬度处存在的平均状况，并根据以下假定确定。标准海平面条件为：

## 4. 语法解析

### ① 插入语 however + because 从句 + than 引导的倒装比较

**原句**：This is not true, however, because pressure drops more rapidly with increased altitude than does the temperature.

**拆解**：主句 This is not true 用破折插入的 however 表示转折；because 引导原因状语从句，从句主干 pressure drops more rapidly（主语 + 谓语 + 比较级状语）；than does the temperature 为比较状语从句，采用“than + 助动词 + 主语”的倒装，does 替代 drops。

**要点**：however 插在两个逗号之间作插入语，不连接句子，翻译时前置为“然而”；than 后的倒装是为了避免头重脚轻，译回中文要还原为“比温度下降得……”。

### ② too…to 与 not enough…to 双重否定式表达

**原句**：Six miles up, for example, it is too thin to support respiration, and 12 miles up, there is not enough oxygen to support combustion, except in some specially designed turbine engine powered airplanes.

**拆解**：主句由 and 连接两个分句；前一分句 it is too thin to support respiration 用 too + 形容词 + to do 表示“太……以致不能……”；后一分句 there is not enough oxygen to support combustion 为 there be 句型，not enough…to do 同样含否定；except in some specially designed turbine engine powered airplanes 为介词短语作例外补充。

**要点**：too…to 与 not enough…to 都暗含否定，译文要显化“无法/不足以”；six miles up、12 miles up 这类高度前置状语译作“在 6 英里高处”。

### ③ at which 定语从句 + the same as that 替代

**原句**：A density altitude of 15,000 ft is the altitude at which the density is the same as that considered standard for 15,000 ft.

**拆解**：主句为 A density altitude of 15,000 ft is the altitude；at which the density is the same as … 为“介词 + 关系代词”引导的定语从句修饰 altitude；the same as 后接 that 替代前文的 the density，considered standard for 15,000 ft 为过去分词短语作 that 的后置定语。

**要点**：at which = at the altitude，翻译时还原为“在该高度上”；that 是替代词而非连接词，不能省；be the same as 意为“与……相同”。

### ④ the + 比较级，the + 比较级 + be capable of doing

**原句**：The higher the temperatures, the more water vapor the air is capable of holding, assuming constant pressure.

**拆解**：The higher the temperatures 为省略系动词的比例分句；the more water vapor the air is capable of holding 中，宾语 the more water vapor 前置，主谓为 the air is capable of holding；assuming constant pressure 为现在分词短语作条件状语。

**要点**：比例句后半部分因宾语提前而语序倒装，翻译时按“温度越高，空气能容纳的水蒸气就越多”还原；assuming 在此等于 if we assume，是独立分词状语，不能译成“假设正在”。

### ⑤ Because 原因从句 + it 形式主语 + would 推测

**原句**：Because both temperature and pressure decrease with altitude, it might appear that the density of the atmosphere would remain fairly constant with increased altitude.

**拆解**：Because both temperature and pressure decrease with altitude 为原因状语从句，主语由 both…and 连接的两项并列构成；主句中 it 为形式主语，真正主语是 that 引导的主语从句；从句内部 the density of the atmosphere would remain fairly constant 为“主语 + 情态动词 + 系动词 + 表语”。

**要点**：might appear that … 表示“看起来似乎……”，后文常用 This is not true 反驳，属技术文章常见的先立后破写法；with increased altitude 是“随着高度增加”，不能译成“与增加的高度一起”。

### ⑥ not…but 并列 + 过去分词后置定语 + as…as 比较

**原句**：Moisture does not consist of tiny particles of liquid held in suspension in the air as in the case of fog, but is an invisible vapor truly as gaseous as the air with which it mixes.

**拆解**：谓语由 does not consist of … but is … 构成 not…but 的并列转折；held in suspension in the air 为过去分词短语作 particles 的后置定语；as in the case of fog 为省略式比较状语；as gaseous as the air 为同级比较，with which it mixes 为“介词 + 关系代词”定语从句修饰 the air。

**要点**：not…but 连接的是两个对等的谓语部分，译文用“并不……而是……”；held in suspension 意为“被悬浮着”，是固定搭配；with which it mixes 还原为“与它混合的空气”。
