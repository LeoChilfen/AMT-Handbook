## 1. 原文

Around 1487, Leonardo da Vinci began research in the area of anthropometrics. The Vitruvian Man, one of his most famous drawings, can be described as one of the earliest sources presenting guidelines for anthropometry. Around the same time, he also began to study the flight of birds. He grasped that humans are too heavy and not strong enough to fly using wings simply attached to the arms. Therefore, he sketched a device in which the aviator lies down on a plank and works two large, membranous wings using hand levers, foot pedals, and a system of pulleys. Today, anthropometry plays a considerable role in the fields of computer design, design for access and maintainability, simplicity of instructions, and ergonomic issues.

In the early 1900s, industrial engineers Frank and Lillian Gilbreth were trying to reduce human error in medicine. They developed the concept of using call backs when communicating in the operating room. For example, the doctor says “scalpel” and the nurse repeats “scalpel” and then hands it to the doctor. That is called the challenge-response system. Speaking out loud reinforces what tool is needed and provides the doctor with an opportunity to make corrections if it is not the necessary tool. This same verbal protocol is used in aviation today.

Prior to World War I, the only test of human to machine compatibility was that of trial and error. If the human functioned with the machine, he was accepted, if not he was rejected. There was a significant change in the concern for humans during the American Civil War. The U.S. Patent Office was concerned about whether the mass-produced uniforms and new weapons could be used effectively by the infantry men.

### Evolution of Maintenance Human Factors

With the onset of World War I (1914–1918), more sophisticated equipment was being developed and the inability of personnel to use such systems led to an increased interest in human capability. Up to this point, the focus of aviation psychology was on the pilot, but as time progressed, the focus shifted onto the aircraft. Of particular concern was the design of the controls and displays, the effects of altitude, and environmental factors on the pilot. The war also brought on the need for aeromedical research and the need for testing and measurement methods. By the end of World War I, two aeronautical labs were established, one at Brooks Air Force Base, Texas, and the other at Wright Field outside of Dayton, Ohio. Another significant development was in the civilian sector, where the effects of illumination on worker productivity were examined. This led to the identification of the Hawthorne Effect, which suggested that motivational factors could significantly influence human performance.

With the onset of World War II (1939–1945), it was becoming increasingly harder to match individuals to pre-existing jobs. Now the design of equipment had to take into account human limitations and take advantage of human capabilities. This change took time as there was a lot of research still to be done to determine the human capabilities and limitations. An example of this is the 1947 study done by Fitts and Jones on the most effective configuration of control knobs to be used in aircraft flight decks. Much of this research transitioned into other equipment with the aim of making the controls and displays easier for the operators to use. Unfortunately, all the “lessons learned” in the WWII studies of group dynamics, and flight crew communication were seemingly forgotten after the war. Post WWII aircrew studies continued to focus primarily on flight crews, especially pilot selection, simulator training, and cockpit layout and design.

Subsequent studies of the technician focused on individual competency and included equipment design (ergonomics). The Vietnam Conflict brought the quest for greater safety, and with that, came a systematic approach for error reduction. This increased attention brought both good and bad changes. It led to the “Zero Defects” quality programs in maintenance and manufacturing. Generally, this had a positive effect. However, it also led to “crackdown programs” which were one-way communication from management (the infamous “my way or the highway” approach). This concept is more over the dunes at Kitty Hawk with their Wright Flyer. dictatorial than democratic, and typically had a long-term negative effect on the company. This “crackdown” approach for behavior control is based upon fear and punishment, which creates a problem. Errors are driven into hiding, and then become apparent later, usually at a more critical time (“Murphy’s Law”). Additional attempts to develop “foolproof” equipment designs were added to the zero-defect manufacturing goal and began to find recognition in the maintenance world as well. Subsequent efforts focused on effects of positive rather than negative motivators. The results of this effort were a reversal of the “crackdown” method, and motivation due to increased morale often improved maintenance safety performance. Studies have shown that motivation resulting from negative sources seldom achieved the same effect. This led to a “Participative Management” style recognized by some U.S. industry and a few airlines, but did not reach maintenance operations until much later.

The Airline Deregulation (1978 –1988) effort had a profound effect upon the aviation community. Prior to 1978, the airline industry was regulated by the Civil Aeronautics Act of 1938. This resulted in peaceful markets, stable routes, and consistent air fares. However, there was a downside consisting of two major problems: wasteful management practices and excessively high wages compared to other comparable skilled-labor industries. The Airline Deregulation Act brought in competitive business practices, with routes and fares controlled by their profitability. This led to a new style of airline management in which a CEO was more of a business person and less knowledgeable of aviation. Existing airlines developed new routes and added new kinds of service and style. Start-up airlines brought other innovative ideas. The numerous mergers and acquisitions added an increasing pressure to focus on the financial bottom line. Doing more with less became the byline. In the 1980s, maintenance departments were not immune to the pressures of mergers and staff reductions. However, fleets were extremely reliable at that time, and significant savings were aided by a reduction in number of maintenance technicians. Other new ways of conducting business included leasing of aircraft and outsourcing of maintenance. A result of deregulation was change for the maintenance programs (both personnel and departmental) and the pressure to produce and adjust. The problem, however, was that human factors for aviation maintenance was still stuck in the 1960s model.

They also should consider their mental state, cognitive capacity, and conditions that may affect their interaction with others. In most cases, human factors programs are designed around the people in the company’s existing workforce. You cannot apply identical strength, size, endurance, experience, motivation, and certification standards equally to all employees. The company must match the physical characteristics of each person to the tasks each performs.

The company must consider factors like each person’s size, strength, age, eyesight, and more to ensure each person is physically capable of performing all the tasks making up the job. A good human factors program considers the limitations of humans and designs the job accordingly. An important element when incorporating human factors into job design is planned rest breaks. People can suffer physical and mental fatigue under many work conditions. Adequate breaks and rest periods ensure the strain of the task does not overload their capabilities. Another “People” consideration, which also is related to “E” for “Environment,” is ensuring there is proper lighting for the task, especially for older workers. Annual vision testing and hearing exams are excellent proactive interventions to ensure optimal human physical performance. Attention to the individual does not stop at physical abilities. A good human factors program must address physiological and psychological factors that affect performance. Companies should do their best to foster good physical and mental health. Offering educational programs on health and fitness is one way to encourage good health. Many companies have reduced sick leave and increased productivity by making healthy meals, snacks, and drinks available to their employees. Companies also should have programs to address issues associated with chemical dependence, including tobacco and alcohol. Another “People” issue involves teamwork and communication. Safe and efficient companies find ways to foster communication and cooperation among workers, managers, and owners. For example, workers should be rewarded for finding ways to improve the system, eliminate waste, and help ensure continuing safety. Environment There are at least two environments in aviation maintenance. There is the physical workplace on the ramp, in the hangar, or in the shop. In addition, there is the organizational environment that exists within the company. A human factors program must pay attention to both environments.

A detailed review of aviation literature published between 1976 and 1987 had very little to say about maintenance. Out of 50 published articles, only 15 even mention maintenance. Most of these articles deal with ergonomics, one article examines military engine design to “solider proof” the maintenance duties, and one U.S. Navy article advocated more management control. As human factors awareness progressed, a “culture change” occurred in U.S. carriers in the 1990s. Management behavior began to change; there were practical applications of systems thinking; organization structure was revised; and new strategy, policy, and values emerged. Virtually all of these involved communication and collaboration. One example is in 1991, when Continental Airlines began “CRM type” training in maintenance. They saw the importance of improving communication, teamwork, and participative decision making. A second example is when United Airlines instituted a change in organization and the job of design of inspectors. They remained more accessible during heavy maintenance and overhaul and stayed in closer communication with mechanics during normal repairs. This resulted in fewer turnbacks and higher quality. A third example is when Southwest Airlines created and sustained a strong and clear organizational structure led by the CEO. This resulted in open and positive communication between the maintenance and other departments. A final example is when TWA instituted a new program to improve communication between the maintenance trade union and maintenance management. This resulted in improved quality.

## 2. 中文翻译

约 1487 年，Leonardo da Vinci 开始研究人体测量学领域。他最著名的画作之一《维特鲁威人》可以描述为最早给出人体测量学准则的文献之一。大约同一时期，他也开始研究鸟类的飞行。他认识到，人太重、也不够强壮，无法仅靠绑在手臂上的翅膀飞行。因此，他画了一种装置：飞行者俯卧在一块木板上，用手柄、脚踏板和滑轮系统操纵两只巨大的膜状翅膀。如今，人体测量学在计算机设计、可达性与可维修性设计、说明书简明性以及人机工程学问题等领域发挥着相当重要的作用。

20 世纪初，工业工程师 Frank 和 Lillian Gilbreth 试图减少医疗中的人为差错。他们提出了在手术室沟通时使用复诵（call back）的概念。例如，医生说「手术刀」，护士重复「手术刀」，然后把刀递给医生。这被称为「挑战—应答系统」。大声说出所需器械，既强化了对所需工具的确认，也让医生在器械不对时有机会纠正。如今航空界也采用同样的口头规程。

### 维修人为因素的演变

随着第一次世界大战（1914—1918）爆发，更复杂的装备不断被研制出来，而人员无法使用这些系统，促使人们更加关注人的能力。至此，航空心理学关注的重点是飞行员，但随着时间的推移，重点转向了飞机。特别受关注的是操纵装置与显示器的设计、高度的影响，以及环境因素对飞行员的影响。战争还带来了航空医学研究的需要，以及测试与测量方法的需要。到一战结束时，已建立两个航空实验室，一个在得克萨斯州 Brooks 空军基地，另一个在俄亥俄州代顿郊外的 Wright Field。另一项重要进展发生在民用领域：人们考察了照明对工人生产率的影响。这导致「霍桑效应」的发现，它表明激励因素能显著影响人的绩效。

随着第二次世界大战（1939—1945）爆发，把个体与既有岗位相匹配变得越来越困难。此时设备设计必须考虑人的局限并利用人的能力。这一转变需要时间，因为仍有许多研究要做，以确定人的能力与局限。一个例子是 1947 年 Fitts 和 Jones 对飞机驾驶舱操纵旋钮最有效构型的研究。这项研究的大部分成果被转移到其他设备上，目的是使操纵装置和显示器更便于操作者使用。不幸的是，二战中关于群体动力学和飞行机组沟通的所有「经验教训」，在战后似乎都被遗忘了。二战后的机组研究继续主要关注飞行机组，尤其是飞行员选拔、模拟机训练以及驾驶舱布局与设计。

随后对技术员的研究侧重于个人能力，并包括设备设计（人机工程学）。越南战争带来了对更高安全性的追求，随之而来的是系统化的差错减少方法。这种更多关注带来了好的和坏的变化。它促成了维修与制造领域的「零缺陷」质量计划。总体而言，这产生了积极影响。然而，它也催生了「严打计划」，即来自管理层的单向沟通（臭名昭著的「按我说的做，否则走人」）。这一概念更……（原文此处插入「在基蒂霍克沙丘上空驾驶莱特飞行者号」的残句）……偏向独裁而非民主，通常会对公司产生长期的负面效果。这种用于控制行为的「严打」做法建立在恐惧与惩罚之上，因而会产生问题。差错被驱入隐蔽状态，随后才暴露出来，而且往往是在更关键的时刻（「墨菲定律」）。为「零缺陷」制造目标又增加了研制「防呆」设备设计的尝试，并开始在维修界得到认可。此后的努力转向积极激励而非消极激励的效果。这项努力的结果是对「严打」方法的反转，而因士气提高产生的激励常常改善了维修安全绩效。研究表明，来自消极源的激励很少能取得同样效果。这促成了被一些美国企业和少数航空公司认可的「参与式管理」风格，但很久以后才进入维修运作。

航空放松管制（1978—1988）对航空界产生了深远影响。1978 年之前，航空业受 1938 年《民用航空法》监管。这带来了平静的市场、稳定的航线和一致的票价。然而也有弊端，包括两大问题：浪费性的管理做法，以及与其他同类熟练工行业相比过高的工资。《航空放松管制法》引入了竞争性商业做法，航线和票价由其盈利能力决定。这导致了一种新的航空公司管理风格：首席执行官更像是商人，对航空了解较少。现有航空公司开辟新航线，增加新的服务种类和风格。初创航空公司带来了其他创新理念。大量合并与收购增加了关注财务底线的压力。「以更少做更多」成了口号。20 世纪 80 年代，维修部门也未能免受合并与裁员压力的影响。不过，当时机队极其可靠，维修技术员数量的减少带来了可观的节省。其他新的经营方式包括飞机租赁和维修外包。放松管制的结果是维修计划（人员和部门层面）发生变化，并带来生产与调整的压力。然而问题在于，航空维修的人为因素仍停留在 20 世纪 60 年代的模式。

……生理、心理和社会心理因素。这些计划必须关注个体、其身体能力以及影响他们的因素。它们还应考虑人的精神状态、认知能力以及可能影响其与他人互动的条件。在多数情况下，人为因素计划是围绕公司现有员工队伍中的人员设计的。你不能把相同的体力、体型、耐力、经验、动机和资格标准同等适用于所有员工。公司必须把每个人的身体特征与每项任务相匹配。

公司必须考虑每个人的体型、力量、年龄、视力等因素，以确保每个人在身体上有能力完成构成该工作的所有任务。良好的人为因素计划会考虑人的局限并据此设计工作。将人为因素纳入工作设计时的一个重要元素是有计划的休息。在许多工作条件下，人都可能遭受身体和精神疲劳。充足的休息和休整期可确保任务的紧张程度不会超出其能力。另一个「人」方面的考虑（也与「E」即「环境」有关）是确保任务有适当的照明，尤其是对年长员工。年度视力检查和听力检查是极好的主动干预措施，可确保最佳的人体绩效。对个体的关注并不止于身体能力。良好的人为因素计划必须处理影响绩效的生理和心理因素。公司应尽力促进良好的身心健康。提供健康与健身教育项目是鼓励良好健康的一种方式。许多公司通过向员工提供健康的餐食、点心和饮料，减少了病假并提高了生产率。公司还应有计划来处理与化学依赖（包括烟草和酒精）相关的问题。另一个「人」的问题涉及团队协作与沟通。安全高效的公司的会设法促进工人、管理者和所有者之间的沟通与合作。例如，应奖励工人寻找改进系统、消除浪费并帮助确保持续安全的方法。环境 航空维修中至少有两种环境。一种是停机坪、机库或车间里的物理工作场所。此外还有公司内部存在的组织环境。人为因素计划必须同时关注这两种环境。

对 1976 年至 1987 年间发表的航空文献的详细回顾，对维修几乎没有涉及。在 50 篇发表文章中，只有 15 篇提到维修。这些文章大多涉及人机工程学，一篇考察军用发动机设计以使维修工作「防呆」，一篇美国海军文章主张加强管理控制。随着人为因素意识的提高，20 世纪 90 年代美国航空公司发生了「文化变革」。管理行为开始改变；系统思维得到实际应用；组织结构被修订；新的战略、政策和价值观出现。几乎所有这些都涉及沟通与协作。一个例子是 1991 年 Continental Airlines 在维修中开始「CRM 型」培训。他们看到了改善沟通、团队协作和参与式决策的重要性。第二个例子是 United Airlines 对组织结构和检查员工作设计进行变革。他们在重型维修和大修期间更易接触到，并在日常修理期间与机械师保持更密切的沟通。这带来了更少的返修和更高的质量。第三个例子是 Southwest Airlines 创建并维持了由 CEO 领导的强有力且清晰的组织结构。这带来了维修部门与其他部门之间公开、积极的沟通。最后一个例子是 TWA 实施了一项新计划，以改善维修工会与维修管理层之间的沟通。这带来了质量提升。

## 4. 语法解析

### ① 同位语与现在分词定语
**原句**：The Vitruvian Man, one of his most famous drawings, can be described as one of the earliest sources presenting guidelines for anthropometry.
**拆解**：主语 The Vitruvian Man 后接同位语 one of his most famous drawings；谓语为 can be described as；presenting guidelines… 为现在分词短语修饰 sources。
**要点**：be described as 意为「可被描述为」；presenting 相当于 which present，分词作定语时不用考虑单复数。

### ② in which 定语从句与 using 分词状语
**原句**：Therefore, he sketched a device in which the aviator lies down on a plank and works two large, membranous wings using hand levers, foot pedals, and a system of pulleys.
**拆解**：主句 he sketched a device；in which 引导定语从句，从句主语 the aviator，并列谓语 lies down 与 works；using… 为现在分词短语作方式状语。
**要点**：in which 相当于 where，先行词是 device；works 在此为及物动词「操纵」。

### ③ if not 的省略与并列条件句
**原句**：If the human functioned with the machine, he was accepted, if not he was rejected.
**拆解**：两个并列的条件句：If the human functioned with the machine, he was accepted 与 if not he was rejected，后者省略了与前者相同的部分。
**要点**：if not 是 if he did not function with the machine 的省略形式；主句均用一般过去时，描述过去的检验做法。

### ④ it 形式主语与 match…to…
**原句**：With the onset of World War II (1939–1945), it was becoming increasingly harder to match individuals to pre-existing jobs.
**拆解**：With… 为时间状语；it 为形式主语，真正主语是 to match individuals to pre-existing jobs；harder 为表语。
**要点**：match A to B 表示「把 A 与 B 相匹配」；increasingly 修饰比较级 harder，表示程度不断加深。

### ⑤ which 引导非限制性定语从句指代整句
**原句**：This “crackdown” approach for behavior control is based upon fear and punishment, which creates a problem.
**拆解**：主句为 This approach is based upon fear and punishment；which 引导非限制性定语从句，指代前面整个主句的内容。
**要点**：非限制性定语从句用逗号隔开，which 不能换成 that；be based upon 意为「建立在……之上」。
